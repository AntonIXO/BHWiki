### Record scope and evidence boundary

**Slug:** `bromelain`

This record treats bromelain as an oral pineapple-derived proteolytic enzyme preparation, not as pineapple fruit or juice. Evidence from bromelain-containing mixtures, papain, serrapeptase, pancreatic enzyme-replacement products, and other prescription proteolytic enzymes is not attributed to bromelain alone. The FDA/EMA prescription burn product NEXOBRID is discussed only to define regulatory and pharmacovigilance boundaries; it is a topical anacaulase-bcdb mixture, not an oral bromelain supplement. [ref-ema-nexobrid]

**Overall evidence judgment.** Human studies support low-level oral absorption, but they do not establish unrestricted or clinically meaningful free systemic protease activity. Oral clinical evidence is most substantial for postoperative pain/swelling, especially after third-molar surgery, yet results are inconsistent and generally more favorable for pain, rescue-analgesic use, or quality-of-life than for objective edema/trismus. Knee-osteoarthritis evidence is conflicting; exercise/recovery evidence is largely null; rhinosinusitis evidence is mostly old or uncontrolled; and modern evidence for general “digestion” or gastrointestinal symptom claims is insufficient. [R2–R6]

A broad 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis found a small pooled advantage for oral bromelain on pain scores, MD −0.27 (95% CI −0.45 to −0.08; nine studies; I²=29%), but the underlying trials spanned different indications and preparations. It characterized the oral-pain evidence as moderate quality rather than establishing broad anti-inflammatory efficacy. [dr-bromelain-s5]

### Human pharmacokinetics, formulations, and exact doses

The clearest absorption study, Castell et al. 1997, involved 19 healthy men. Bromelain was detected after oral administration by immunologic methods and by residual proteolytic activity. During multidose exposure at 3 g/day, plasma concentrations reached up to approximately 5,000 pg/mL by 48 hours; the estimated plasma half-life was 6–9 hours, and an estimated mean 10.8 μg of bromelain was present in plasma over the 3–51-hour interval. Circulating bromelain was associated with α2-macroglobulin and α1-antichymotrypsin. Intact or near-intact protein can therefore cross the gut in small amounts, but inhibitor binding and very low systemic concentrations mean this does not prove clinically important free systemic enzymatic action. [dr-bromelain-s2]

The German Bromelain-POS technical information similarly states that intestinal absorption produces dose-dependent picomolar plasma concentrations, that bromelain binds α2-macroglobulin, and that elimination occurs rapidly through the liver; it also states that product-specific pharmacokinetic data for Bromelain-POS itself are unavailable. [dr-bromelain-s7]

Formulation matters. Bromelain-POS is gastro-resistant/enteric-coated. One tablet contains 56.25–95 mg bromelain corresponding to exactly 500 FIP units. The broad mass interval for a fixed activity value demonstrates why bromelain milligrams are not a reliable substitute for proteolytic activity units. Its labeled adult/adolescent regimen is one 500-FIP tablet once or twice daily, swallowed whole with ample fluid about 30 minutes before food; without medical advice the product information limits use to 4–5 days. [dr-bromelain-s7]

FIP and GDU should not be converted by assumption. They are activity measurements obtained using particular enzyme assays, whereas “mg” is preparation mass. Unless a tested preparation reports both measures under specified assay conditions, a trial reported as 500 mg/day cannot safely be restated as a FIP or GDU dose. A recent dental study separately characterized purified bromelain and pineapple extract with substantially different enzyme activities, reinforcing the need to keep purified bromelain distinct from pineapple extract. [dr-bromelain-s8]

### Clinical efficacy and biomarker evidence

For general inflammation, biomarkers and patient outcomes must remain separate. The 2023 Pereira systematic review identified seven randomized clinical trials, with isolated-bromelain regimens roughly 200–1,050 mg/day for one to sixteen weeks. Biomarkers included inflammatory mediators and oxidative/inflammatory measurements, but results were heterogeneous and isolated positive findings did not form a consistent systemic anti-inflammatory pattern. The broader Leelakanok meta-analysis supports a small pain signal rather than a validated generalized anti-inflammatory indication. [R21, R5]

For knee osteoarthritis, the strongest placebo-controlled study is negative. Brien et al. randomized 47 people with moderate-to-severe knee OA to 800 mg/day bromelain or placebo for 12 weeks; only 31 completed. There was no significant between-group difference for total WOMAC, WOMAC subscales, or SF-36. The primary estimate was nonsignificant (coefficient 11.16; p=0.27; 95% CI −8.86 to 31.18). [dr-bromelain-s3]

Kasemsuk et al. randomized 40 patients with mild-to-moderate knee OA to 500 mg/day bromelain or 100 mg/day diclofenac. At four weeks, WOMAC and SF-36 outcomes did not significantly differ. Patients remaining on bromelain showed better WOMAC and physical SF-36 values at week 16 versus their own baseline, but this later phase was not placebo-controlled and cannot establish independent efficacy. [dr-bromelain-s9]

Measured OA biomarkers in that study were more suggestive than the comparative clinical endpoint: plasma malondialdehyde and lipopolysaccharide-stimulated whole-blood PGE2 decreased after 16 weeks. Those laboratory changes are not evidence of cartilage preservation or disease modification. [dr-bromelain-s9]

For postoperative dental pain and swelling, direction varies by outcome. In a 34-patient double-blind placebo-controlled trial, de la Barrera-Núñez et al. found no statistically significant differences in inflammation, pain, oral aperture, or related endpoints, although numerical trends favored bromelain for some measures. [dr-bromelain-s11]

Majid and Al-Mashhadani randomized 45 third-molar patients among bromelain, diclofenac, and placebo. Pain was significantly lower with bromelain than placebo, and quality-of-life measures improved, whereas the bromelain reduction in swelling and trismus did not reach statistical significance. A positive pain/QoL result should not be rewritten as proven anti-edema efficacy. [dr-bromelain-s12]

Bormann et al. used objective 3D facial scanning in a crossover dose-finding study. Neither 3,000 nor 4,500 FIP/day demonstrated superiority over 1,000 FIP/day. Pooled bromelain periods showed favorable trends versus placebo, but the absence of a higher-dose advantage argues against assuming a simple dose-response relationship. Adverse-event frequency was not higher under bromelain. [dr-bromelain-s13]

An active-comparator triple-blind study by Gupta et al. reported less edema and trismus with bromelain than aceclofenac and similar analgesic efficacy, but without a placebo group it provides comparative rather than placebo-controlled efficacy evidence. [ref-colletti-2024]

Systematic reviews of third-molar studies consequently do not produce a single uniform result. Mendes et al. found modest pain and quality-of-life benefits while finding no convincing facial-swelling or trismus effect, whereas other meta-analyses estimated small edema effects. The most defensible synthesis is a modest, low-to-moderate-certainty signal for pain/rescue analgesia or patient-centered recovery, with less consistent objective swelling/trismus effects. [R5, R11, R12, R13]

A 2026 non-dental postoperative RCT adds one positive signal. Shamloo et al. randomized 46 upper-blepharoplasty patients to 200 mg (500 GDU) every eight hours for two weeks or matching placebo. Photographically graded edema was significantly lower on postoperative days 3, 7, and 11; ecchymosis differed on day 3 but not days 7 or 11, and adverse effects did not significantly differ. This small single-center study requires replication. [dr-bromelain-s15]

For sinusitis and respiratory claims, evidence is weaker. A 2023 meta-review judged bromelain potentially useful for sinusitis, but the evidence consists largely of older small trials and later uncontrolled/observational studies; NCCIH characterizes the number of oral bromelain studies for sinusitis as small. No comparably persuasive bromelain-monotherapy evidence supports asthma, bronchitis, or routine upper-respiratory infection claims. [R16, R27]

For digestion and gastrointestinal claims, no robust modern bromelain-alone evidence was identified for generic “improved digestion,” dyspepsia, bloating, or protein digestion in otherwise typical consumers. Historical pancreatic-insufficiency trials generally used multi-enzyme products and cannot be attributed to bromelain alone. ClinicalTrials.gov lists NCT06351696, comparing bromelain with placebo in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis alongside a low-FODMAP dietary intervention; the retrieved registry record had no posted efficacy results, so it cannot support a treatment claim. [dr-bromelain-s6]

For exercise/recovery, controlled results are mostly negative. Stone et al. found that 900 mg/day bromelain did not alter delayed-onset muscle-soreness pain, range-of-motion loss, or peak concentric torque after eccentric exercise. [ref-castell-1997] In a six-day competitive cycling study of 15 trained cyclists, 1,000 mg/day failed to prevent rises in creatine kinase, LDH, or myoglobin; testosterone showed only a borderline treatment signal, and subjective fatigue differed on one race day. These findings are insufficient to establish faster muscular recovery. [ref-leelakanok-2023]

### Adverse effects, coagulation, interactions, and special populations

The best directly usable oral medicinal-product safety information comes from Bromelain-POS. Its technical information contraindicates use in people hypersensitive to bromelain or pineapple. It classifies allergic reactions, including rash or asthma-like symptoms, as common (≥1/100 to <1/10), gastrointestinal discomfort and/or diarrhea as occasional (≥1/1,000 to <1/100), and prolonged bleeding time as very rare (<1/10,000). These are label frequency categories for this specific medicinal product, not reliable incidence estimates for every commercial bromelain supplement. [dr-bromelain-s7]

For platelet/coagulation and bleeding risk, direct modern oral RCT evidence quantifying clinical bleeding is sparse. The German product information nevertheless says Bromelain-POS should not be used in patients with coagulation disorders or those receiving anticoagulants or platelet-aggregation inhibitors, states that concurrent use may increase bleeding tendency, and advises stopping the product before an operation because it influences blood coagulation. It does not provide an evidence-based universal number of preoperative washout days. [dr-bromelain-s7]

Human in-vitro platelet experiments and animal thrombosis models were not used to infer that ordinary oral dosing produces a clinically important antiplatelet effect in vivo. Current clinical evidence supports a precautionary interaction signal whose magnitude is unquantified, rather than a measured warfarin-like anticoagulant effect.

The Bromelain-POS information states that bromelain may increase absorption of other drugs and that plasma and urine antibiotic concentrations increase during concurrent treatment; an older human study specifically investigated oral tetracycline absorption with bromelain. The same label explicitly states that systematic interaction studies are lacking and the mechanism remains unknown. [R7, R18]

No qualifying dedicated human interaction trial was identified that quantifies bromelain's effect with warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, clopidogrel, or non-aspirin NSAIDs. Comparator trials against diclofenac, ibuprofen, or aceclofenac do not answer the safety question of taking those drugs together with bromelain. Aspirin remains relevant because of its antiplatelet action; it falls under the class-level bleeding caution in a way that non-aspirin NSAID comparator trials do not resolve. [dr-bromelain-s7]

For renal and hepatic impairment, Bromelain-POS states that no dose reduction is required in mild-to-moderate renal or hepatic impairment, but relevant laboratory parameters should be monitored in severe impairment. Because product-specific PK data are unavailable, this guidance should not be generalized to all bromelain formulations. [dr-bromelain-s7]

For pregnancy and lactation, evidence is inadequate rather than reassuring. The oral product information states that there are no pregnancy-use data, reproductive-toxicity data are inadequate, use during pregnancy is not recommended, passage of bromelain components or metabolites into human milk is unknown, and use while breastfeeding is not recommended. No qualifying controlled human pregnancy or lactation study was found. [dr-bromelain-s7]

The FDA-approved topical prescription product NEXOBRID must remain separate. NEXOBRID is anacaulase-bcdb, a mixture of pineapple-stem proteolytic enzymes used topically for burn-eschar removal. Its FDA label contraindicates known hypersensitivity to anacaulase-bcdb, bromelain or pineapples, and separately lists papaya/papain hypersensitivity because of possible cross-sensitivity; postmarketing experience includes serious hypersensitivity, including anaphylaxis and urticaria. It also carries a coagulation warning. These are regulatory safety signals, not oral-bromelain incidence estimates. [R1, R19]

### Regulatory status, sponsorship, and evidence quality

In the United States, oral bromelain sold as a dietary supplement is not FDA-approved for safety or effectiveness before sale. FDA states that dietary-supplement manufacturers and distributors are responsible for evaluating safety and labeling before marketing, while FDA can act against adulterated or misbranded products after marketing. This is categorically different from FDA approval of topical prescription NEXOBRID, whose U.S. label records initial approval in 2022. [R19, R20]

In Germany, Bromelain-POS is an enteric-coated pharmacy-only medicinal product. Its technical information gives authorization number 70577.00.00, initial authorization on June 2, 2009, renewal on October 23, 2015, and an information revision date of June 2018. Its labeled indication is adjunctive treatment of acute swelling after operations and injuries, particularly involving the nose and paranasal sinuses. [dr-bromelain-s7]

The sponsorship record is uneven. Many older bromelain studies predate modern disclosure standards, so absence of a disclosed sponsor or conflict is unknown, not “none.” PubMed identifies several studies as receiving non-U.S.-government research support without exposing a specific funder in the abstract metadata. [R3, R4] Recent work sometimes provides clearer disclosures: Colletti et al. explicitly reported no conflicts of interest, and the accepted 2026 blepharoplasty manuscript likewise reported no potential conflicts; funding details were not fully recoverable in the sources used here. [R8, R15]

The combination-enzyme literature has identifiable commercial involvement and was excluded from bromelain-alone efficacy attribution. NCT05038410 studies bromelain + trypsin + rutoside in osteoarthritis and lists Mucos Pharma GmbH & Co. KG as lead sponsor with Société des Produits Nestlé and Artialis as collaborators. It cannot answer whether bromelain itself caused any pharmacodynamic or clinical effect. [dr-bromelain-s17]

### Open questions and limitations

The main unresolved evidence gaps are product standardization, absence of validated mg↔FIP↔GDU equivalence, poor sponsor/COI reporting in older studies, sparse dedicated drug-interaction trials, no validated universal surgical washout interval, little evidence in severe renal/hepatic impairment, and absence of adequate human pregnancy/lactation evidence. These gaps materially limit attempts to generalize one bromelain preparation, dose, or safety profile to another. [dr-bromelain-s7]

## Mechanisms

```yaml
- title: Heterogeneous cysteine-protease activity
  description: Pineapple-stem bromelain is not one enzyme. Biochemical fractionation resolved multiple catalytically active papain-family cysteine proteases with different physicochemical and substrate properties. Proteolysis is therefore a core biochemical mechanism, but the activity of a commercial preparation depends on its protease composition and assay.
  sourceId: ref-matagne-2017
  conceptId: cysteine-protease-activity
- title: Human inflammatory-tissue cytokine modulation ex vivo
  description: Endoscopic colon biopsies from patients with ulcerative colitis or Crohn disease were treated with bromelain ex vivo. Inflamed tissue showed decreased secretion of several inflammatory mediators, including G-CSF, GM-CSF, IFN-gamma, CCL4/MIP-1beta and TNF. This human-tissue experiment supports biological plausibility but is not equivalent to demonstrating the same effect after an oral clinical dose.
  sourceId: ref-onken-2008
  conceptId: immune-inflammatory-modulation
- title: NF-kappaB and MAPK modulation in human cells
  description: In LPS-stimulated human dental-pulp cells, bromelain reduced IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 and reduced NF-kappaB p65, ERK and p38 signaling. This is an in-vitro human-cell mechanism and should not be treated as proof of a clinical anti-inflammatory pathway at oral supplement concentrations.
  sourceId: ref-hong-2021
  conceptId: immune-inflammatory-modulation
- title: Platelet and coagulation modulation in laboratory systems
  description: Human platelet and whole-blood experiments show that bromelain can reduce aggregation and alter coagulation measures ex vivo. A thromboelastography study also found complex and partly paradoxical findings in vivo in mice, emphasizing that laboratory anticoagulant effects do not establish a quantified clinical bleeding effect after oral use.
  sourceId: ref-kaur-2016
  conceptId: hemostasis-modulation
- title: Product-specific enzymatic burn-eschar digestion
  description: The current NexoBrid label states that its proteolytic-enzyme mixture dissolves burn-wound eschar, while the specific individual components responsible have not been identified. This mechanism applies to topical anacaulase-bcdb placed directly on burn eschar and is not evidence that oral bromelain debrides wounds.
  sourceId: ref-dailymed-nexobrid-2025
  conceptId: enzymatic-eschar-debridement
```

## Cautions

```yaml
- title: Bromelain, pineapple and papain/papaya hypersensitivity
  description: IgE-mediated occupational reactions to bromelain have been reported. The current NexoBrid label contraindicates use in patients with known hypersensitivity to anacaulase-bcdb, bromelain, pineapple or other product components and also in patients with papaya/papain hypersensitivity because of cross-sensitivity risk. Postmarketing serious hypersensitivity, including anaphylaxis, has been reported for NexoBrid.
  sourceId: ref-dailymed-nexobrid-2025
- title: Bleeding and coagulation uncertainty
  description: Platelet inhibition, fibrinolytic effects and changes in coagulation measures are supported mainly by in-vitro, ex-vivo and animal evidence. The current NexoBrid label advises avoiding use in uncontrolled coagulation disorders and using caution with anticoagulant therapy or other bleeding risks. These sources do not establish a numerical major-bleeding incidence for routine oral bromelain supplementation.
  sourceId: ref-dailymed-nexobrid-2025
- title: Pregnancy evidence is inadequate
  description: Direct human pregnancy safety data for oral bromelain are inadequate. The current NexoBrid label states that human pregnancy data are unavailable; its animal intravenous developmental studies are product- and route-specific and cannot establish oral-supplement safety.
  sourceId: ref-dailymed-nexobrid-2025
- title: Lactation evidence is inadequate
  description: Human milk transfer and effects on breastfed infants have not been established for bromelain. The current NexoBrid label likewise reports no data on anacaulase-bcdb in milk or its effects on the infant or milk production.
  sourceId: ref-dailymed-nexobrid-2025
- title: Product identity and potency can be unreliable
  description: A 2026 analytical study of four commercial bromelain raw materials found two without meaningful enzymatic activity and one whose apparent compliant proteolytic activity was attributable to papain rather than bromelain. Activity units alone therefore do not prove bromelain identity or purity.
  sourceId: ref-benetti-2026
- title: Long-term safety and rare adverse events remain insufficiently characterized
  description: Most controlled oral studies are small and short. Short-term tolerability cannot exclude rare allergic, bleeding, gastrointestinal or other adverse events, and long-duration high-dose safety has not been adequately established.
  sourceId: ref-leelakanok-2023
- title: Dependence, tolerance and withdrawal are not established clinical phenomena
  description: The reviewed bromelain clinical literature does not provide a developed evidence base for pharmacologic dependence, a reproducible withdrawal syndrome or clinically meaningful tolerance. This is an evidence gap rather than proof that every long-term exposure is risk-free.
  sourceId: ref-leelakanok-2023
```
