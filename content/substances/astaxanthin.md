## Summary

Astaxanthin is a xanthophyll carotenoid with formulation-sensitive human pharmacokinetics. Clinical findings differ by skin, visual, exercise and metabolic endpoint; antioxidant biomarkers do not establish a general clinical benefit.

Deep Research synthesis: Astaxanthin — returned Deep Research evidence record.

## Description

Human studies use free or esterified astaxanthin, algal extracts, oils and proprietary mixtures. Formulation, meal context and isomer composition affect exposure. Clinical findings include positive and null results, with small samples and combination-product attribution limits.

## Evidence note

Selected primary studies and reviews, not an independently reviewed systematic assessment. Several trials involve manufacturers; declarations remain attached to each reference. Plasma exposure, clinical outcomes and felt-effect timing are distinct, and unknown molecular identity or disclosures remain unassessed.

### Astaxanthin

Slug: astaxanthin
Evidence cutoff: 2026-10-09

### Scope and bottom line

Astaxanthin is best characterized as a lipid-soluble carotenoid supplement with strongly formulation-dependent exposure, not as an established treatment for any disease. Across the human evidence reviewed, the strongest recurring healthy-person signal is for some skin hydration/elasticity measures; a small UV-challenge RCT also found an increased change in minimal erythema dose. However, the skin meta-analysis found no significant reduction in wrinkle depth, and much of this literature is small, heterogeneous, and commercially entangled. [dr-astaxanthin-s1][ref-zhou-2021]

For eye/retinal outcomes, one 2025 pediatric trial of 4 mg/day reported improved digital-eye-strain symptoms, but several objective ophthalmic endpoints were null. Adult studies often combine astaxanthin with lutein/zeaxanthin, making astaxanthin-specific attribution impossible. No robust qualifying astaxanthin-alone evidence establishes prevention or treatment of macular degeneration, retinal disease, or myopia. [dr-astaxanthin-s3][ref-ito-2018]

For cognition/nootropic use, a 96-person trial of 6 or 12 mg/day reported within-group improvements but explicitly acknowledged that it was too small to demonstrate significant cognitive superiority over placebo. A 2024 meta-analysis found cognitive accuracy only marginally different and reaction time non-significant. [dr-astaxanthin-s5][ref-hecht-2025]

For exercise, the newest broad synthesis materially weakens the ergogenic claim: a 2026 meta-analysis of 24 RCTs found no significant effect on VO₂max, time-trial performance, or maximal workload/power. Creatine kinase fell modestly, while LDH was less robust and MDA/IL-6 were null; those biochemical findings cannot be translated into proven faster functional recovery or better athletic performance. [dr-astaxanthin-s7]

For metabolic/cardiovascular disease, pooled results are inconsistent and largely restricted to surrogate biomarkers. A 2020 meta-analysis was null for fasting glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, body weight/BMI, and blood pressure, with only a small HDL-C signal and subgroup CRP findings; a 2024 meta-analysis produced more favorable lipid estimates, demonstrating sensitivity to study selection/methodology. Neither establishes fewer myocardial infarctions, strokes, cardiovascular hospitalizations, or deaths. [dr-astaxanthin-s8][dr-astaxanthin-s9]

Overall BHWiki verdict: evidence supports at most modest, outcome-specific supplement effects, mainly cosmetic/skin and selected biomarkers. It does not support describing astaxanthin as a proven nootropic, ergogenic agent, cardiovascular therapy, diabetes therapy, fertility treatment, retinal-disease therapy, or general “anti-inflammatory/anti-aging” treatment.

### Healthy-person biohacking and supplement claims

### Skin and UV response

The 2021 systematic review/meta-analysis identified 11 human studies, including nine randomized controlled oral studies. Oral astaxanthin improved measured skin moisture, SMD 0.53 (95% CI 0.05–1.01; I² 52%), and elasticity, SMD 0.77 (0.19–1.35; I² 75%), but did not significantly improve wrinkle depth, SMD −0.26 (−0.58 to 0.06). High heterogeneity for elasticity and small underlying studies materially limit certainty. [dr-astaxanthin-s1]

A particularly relevant UV trial randomized 23 healthy Japanese adults and administered 4 mg/day for nine weeks. The product was natural Haematococcus pluvialis astaxanthin, ASTOTS/FUJIFILM, made with supercritical-CO₂ extraction and a dispersant technology intended to increase absorption. Change in minimal erythema dose favored astaxanthin, and loss of skin moisture after one UV-exposure condition was attenuated. However, TEWL was not significantly different, and several subjective skin endpoints were also null. No treatment-related adverse events or serious safety-laboratory changes were reported. [ref-zhou-2021]

That trial deserves a material conflict-of-interest downgrade: the product was a FUJIFILM preparation and the authors were FUJIFILM-affiliated; the publication reported no external funding. A “no external funding” statement therefore does not make this independent replication. [ref-zhou-2021]

Interpretation: plausible modest cosmetic/UV-response benefit, but insufficient evidence for preventing UV-induced disease. An increased erythema threshold is not evidence of protection from DNA damage, photo-carcinogenesis, or a replacement for sunscreen.

### Eye and retinal outcomes

In the 2025 randomized double-blind pediatric digital-eye-strain trial, 64 children aged 10–14 with ≥4 hours/day screen exposure received 4 mg/day astaxanthin soft capsules for 84 days. The astaxanthin group had greater improvement in the Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire and visual-fatigue score, plus selected stereopsis and pupillary-reflex findings. In contrast, there was no significant between-group advantage for visual acuity, spherical equivalent, near point of accommodation, near exophoria, Schirmer I tear production, immune variables, or safety measures. The trial was registered as NCT05602402. [dr-astaxanthin-s3]

An adult VDT trial used a combination of 6 mg astaxanthin, 10 mg lutein, and 2 mg zeaxanthin daily for eight weeks. Eye-hand coordination and macular-pigment optical density changed, but smooth-pursuit eye movement did not clearly improve. Because three carotenoids were administered together, this study cannot establish astaxanthin-specific efficacy; MPOD is also a retinal biomarker, not a clinical retinal-disease outcome. [ref-ito-2018]

Interpretation: limited evidence for digital-eye-strain symptoms; no convincing astaxanthin-alone evidence for treatment or prevention of retinal degeneration or myopia.

### Cognition, fatigue, and “brain optimization”

Katagiri et al. randomized 96 middle-aged/older adults reporting age-related forgetfulness to placebo or H. pluvialis extract supplying 6 or 12 mg astaxanthin/day for 12 weeks. CogHealth scores improved within the 12-mg group and some Groton Maze measures improved earlier in active groups, but the investigators explicitly stated that the study was too small to demonstrate a significant cognitive-function difference between astaxanthin and placebo. No adverse effects were reported. [dr-astaxanthin-s5]

The 2024 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs involving 346 healthy participants estimated cognitive accuracy at SMD 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26), i.e. marginal/non-significant, and reaction time at −0.08 (−0.26 to 0.10), also non-significant. [ref-hecht-2025]

Interpretation: current human evidence does not support a robust nootropic claim.

### Exercise performance and recovery

The 2026 meta-analysis included 24 RCTs in athletes and physically active/healthy populations, with doses spanning approximately 4–28 mg/day and interventions ranging from four days to 16 weeks. Its pooled performance results were:

| Outcome | Pooled effect | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| VO₂max | SMD −0.14, 95% CI −0.78 to 0.50 | Null |
| Time-trial performance | SMD −0.17, −0.76 to 0.42 | Null |
| Maximum workload/power | SMD −0.11, −0.53 to 0.31 | Null |
| Creatine kinase | SMD −0.45, −0.83 to −0.07 | Modest biomarker reduction |
| LDH | SMD −0.93, −1.39 to −0.48 | Favorable but highly heterogeneous |
| MDA | SMD −0.22, −0.59 to 0.14 | Null |
| IL-6 | SMD −0.23, −0.65 to 0.20 | Null |

LDH was especially fragile: substantial heterogeneity was reported, and exclusion of one influential study eliminated the pooled statistical benefit. The review rated CK more credible than the other recovery biomarkers but emphasized that performance outcomes remained non-significant. [dr-astaxanthin-s7]

A useful primary-trial counterexample used 6 mg/day for four weeks in 14 active young men. Whole-blood glutathione was about 7% higher after astaxanthin, but plasma hydrogen peroxide and MDA were unchanged and fat oxidation during graded exercise did not improve. This is precisely why antioxidant-biomarker changes should not be converted into performance claims. [dr-astaxanthin-s8]

A very small 2025 trial in ten physically active men reported longer cycling time to exhaustion after 28 mg/day for four days, with lower CK and MDA, while CRP/TNF and substrate use were null. The isolated positive performance result is outweighed by the substantially larger 2026 pooled analysis and should be considered hypothesis-generating. [dr-astaxanthin-s7]

Interpretation: no reliable ergogenic effect. There may be a modest effect on CK after strenuous exercise, but that is not proof of less soreness, faster return of strength, improved training adaptation, or superior competitive performance.

### Disease-treatment claims and biomarker evidence

### Metabolic and cardiovascular endpoints

The 2020 RCT meta-analysis found no significant effect on fasting glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, BMI, body weight, systolic BP, or diastolic BP. HDL-C rose by approximately 1.47 mg/dL. CRP fell only in subgroup analyses involving ≥12 weeks or >12 mg/day, not as a uniform overall treatment effect. [dr-astaxanthin-s8]

A later 2024 meta-analysis of antioxidant functional lipids generated more favorable astaxanthin subgroup estimates: LDL-C −0.11 mmol/L, HDL-C +0.13 mmol/L, total cholesterol −0.22 mmol/L, triglycerides −0.46 mmol/L, and fasting insulin −2.66 pmol/L. This disagreement with the earlier synthesis indicates that cardiometabolic conclusions are sensitive to included trials and analytic choices. Critically, these remain surrogate risk factors, not cardiovascular-event outcomes. [dr-astaxanthin-s9]

A completed UC San Diego registry trial in insulin-resistant subjects enrolled 34 participants and listed change in insulin sensitivity as its primary outcome, but the reviewed ClinicalTrials.gov record had no posted results. A completed registration is therefore not positive evidence. [dr-astaxanthin-s23]

In patients with established heart failure, a 2026 double-blind trial randomized 80 patients with stage C/D HF and LVEF <50% to 20 mg/day for eight weeks or placebo. TNF-α and MCP-1 fell more with astaxanthin and VLDL declined; hs-CRP, other lipid measures, and anthropometric indices were largely null. The reported endpoints did not establish fewer HF hospitalizations, cardiovascular deaths, or other hard clinical benefits. [ref-katagiri-2012]

Disease-treatment conclusion: insufficient evidence to recommend astaxanthin as treatment for diabetes, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, atherosclerotic disease, or heart failure.

### Inflammation and oxidative-stress biomarkers

The 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis pooled 12 RCTs with 380 participants. Blood MDA was lower overall, SMD −0.95 (95% CI −1.67 to −0.23); a T2DM subgroup also showed lower MDA, and IL-6 in T2DM fell by about 0.70 pg/mL. However, CRP and TNF-α were not significantly reduced, and relatively few trials informed several other biomarkers. [dr-astaxanthin-s12]

That result is not universal across physiological settings: the newer exercise-specific meta-analysis found pooled MDA and IL-6 effects non-significant. [dr-astaxanthin-s7]

Interpretation: astaxanthin may modify selected redox/inflammatory laboratory measurements in some populations, but biomarkers do not demonstrate prevention or treatment of inflammatory disease.

### PCOS, fertility, and reproductive outcomes

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis covering four randomized PCOS trials found increased follicular-fluid total antioxidant capacity but inconsistent effects on MDA, catalase, and superoxide dismutase. Although some oocyte/embryo-quality measures appeared favorable, fertilization and pregnancy outcomes were not significantly improved. [dr-astaxanthin-s13]

Interpretation: no established fertility or PCOS treatment effect.

### Dose, formulation, pharmacokinetics, and product identity

Milligram dose alone is an inadequate description of an astaxanthin intervention. Human studies have used H. pluvialis algal extract, ester-rich oleoresins, oil softgels, proprietary dispersions, micellar products, water-dispersible preparations, and multi-ingredient combinations. EFSA's regulatory record itself distinguishes algal powder, oleoresin and water-dispersible formulations with different astaxanthin concentrations. [dr-astaxanthin-s20][dr-astaxanthin-s21][dr-astaxanthin-s22]

The most directly relevant human PK study of lipid vehicles administered a single 40-mg dose to healthy men. Compared with a reference commercial supplement, formulations based on palm oil or glycerol mono-/dioleate plus surfactants produced approximately 1.7- to 3.7-fold greater bioavailability. The overall reported elimination half-life was 15.9 ± 5.3 hours. The highest exposure occurred with a glycerol mono-/dioleate plus polysorbate-80 formulation. [dr-astaxanthin-s14]

A 2024 randomized crossover PK study makes the product-equivalence problem even clearer. Twelve healthy men received two products each nominally providing 8 mg astaxanthin. Micellar NovaSOL reached a Cmax of 7.21 μg/mL at 3.67 hours, compared with 3.86 μg/mL at 8.5 hours for native/reference astaxanthin. Thus, equal labeled milligrams did not produce equal plasma kinetics. [ref-exercise-meta-2026]

An older three-person experiment administered a single 100-mg dose with a meal containing 74% all-E, 9% 9Z and 17% 13Z astaxanthin. Tmax was 6.7 ± 1.2 hours, estimated half-life 21 ± 11 hours, and much of circulating astaxanthin appeared in VLDL/chylomicron fractions. Because n=3 and the dose was enormous relative to normal supplements, it provides mechanistic PK information rather than routine dosing guidance. [dr-astaxanthin-s16]

The available evidence therefore supports a formulation/vehicle effect on absorption. It is less defensible to turn that into a precise generic instruction such as “take exactly X grams of fat”; the qualifying human evidence here tested particular formulations rather than a dose-response series of meal-fat quantities. [dr-astaxanthin-s14]

ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02397811 is especially revealing for product identity: Cyanotech sponsored a 12-person crossover comparison of six commercial forms—enteric-coated softgel, liposomal, standard softgel, water-soluble emulsion, water-dispersible powder, and standard softgel with astaxanthin gel. The registry reports completion but no posted results. [dr-astaxanthin-s19]

For BHWiki/product comparison, the minimum useful identity fields are therefore: source organism; free versus esterified astaxanthin where specified; astaxanthin milligrams rather than total extract mass; extraction method; carrier oil/excipients; proprietary dispersion/micellar technology; and co-ingredients. The human PK evidence does not support assuming bioequivalence across products. [ref-exercise-meta-2026][dr-astaxanthin-s14]

### Safety, special populations, interactions, and pharmacovigilance

A dedicated randomized safety trial enrolled 35 healthy adults for eight weeks. Treatment was three safflower-oil gelcaps/day, each containing 2 mg astaxanthin = 6 mg/day; placebo was safflower oil alone. Blood pressure, comprehensive metabolic chemistry, and blood counts showed no clinically important treatment-versus-placebo differences. Small statistical differences in serum calcium, total protein and eosinophils were judged clinically unimportant by the investigators. [dr-astaxanthin-s17]

That trial supports short-term tolerability of 6 mg/day in healthy adults, not safety of chronic high-dose use or exclusion of rare adverse effects. Its n=35 and eight-week duration are far too small to characterize uncommon toxicity. [dr-astaxanthin-s17]

A potentially relevant hepatic signal comes from the 2024 meta-analysis of five RCTs, 196 total participants, using 6–12 mg/day for 4–48 weeks. ALT rose by a pooled 1.92 U/L (95% CI 0.16–3.68), while AST, GGT and ALP did not significantly change. The increase is small and its clinical significance is uncertain, but it contradicts a blanket claim that astaxanthin improves liver function or is demonstrably hepatically neutral at all studied doses. [dr-astaxanthin-s18]

Renal impairment: no dedicated qualifying human renal-impairment PK, renal dose-adjustment, or CKD safety trial was identified in this review. The 2018 UV trial measured BUN and creatinine but excluded people with a history of serious kidney dysfunction, so normal short-term safety testing in that population cannot establish CKD safety. [ref-zhou-2021]

Pregnancy and lactation: no dedicated qualifying human supplementation trial in pregnant or lactating people was identified. The 2018 healthy-adult skin trial explicitly excluded pregnancy, lactation, and planned pregnancy. EFSA's food-exposure modeling includes pregnant/lactating adults within broader exposure calculations, but that is not equivalent to a pregnancy safety trial. [ref-zhou-2021][dr-astaxanthin-s21][dr-astaxanthin-s22]

Medication interactions: no dedicated human clinical drug–drug-interaction study was identified in the eligible evidence set. Mechanistic/animal interaction claims were deliberately excluded. Accordingly, specific interactions with anticoagulants, antihypertensives, glucose-lowering drugs, CYP substrates, or other medicines should be labeled unknown/not clinically characterized, rather than presented as established human interactions.

Pharmacovigilance: controlled trials reviewed here are generally too small for rare-event detection. No astaxanthin-specific validated regulator pharmacovigilance signal or regulator-published human case series was identified in the eligible official sources reviewed. This is a data-gap statement, not evidence that rare adverse reactions do not occur.

### Regulatory status, sponsorship, and evidence limitations

### United States

FDA GRN 294 concerns H. pluvialis extract containing astaxanthin esters, submitted by Fuji Chemical Industry. The specified conventional-food uses provide 0.1 mg astaxanthin per serving; FDA closed the notice on January 6, 2010 with “no questions,” while noting that some uses may require color-additive listing. [dr-astaxanthin-s20]

FDA GRN 580, submitted by INNOBIO, covers specified conventional-food categories up to 0.15 mg astaxanthin per serving; FDA closed it in 2015 with the same “no questions” formulation and color-additive caveat. [dr-astaxanthin-s20]

These are GRAS records for defined ingredients and conditions of food use. They are not an FDA drug approval, not evidence of efficacy, and do not mean that every retail astaxanthin supplement or every 4–28 mg/day research regimen has been “FDA approved.” [dr-astaxanthin-s20]

### European Union / EFSA

EFSA's 2020 assessment applied an acceptable daily intake of 0.2 mg/kg body weight/day. Under its modeled background-diet exposure, adding an 8-mg/day supplement was considered safe for adults; adolescents 14–<18 could reach the ADI, while younger age groups could exceed it under that scenario. [dr-astaxanthin-s21]

EFSA's 2025 opinion documents the subsequent EU authorization framework for the specified H. pluvialis astaxanthin-rich oleoresin: up to 2.3 mg/day at ages 3–<10, 5.7 mg/day at ages 10–<14, and 8 mg/day at age ≥14. The label also requires that the product not be consumed on a day when another astaxanthin-containing supplement is consumed; use is not authorized for children under three under that entry. [dr-astaxanthin-s22]

The 2025 application was submitted by AstaReal AB, which requested protection for proprietary data. EFSA's opinion also describes the oleoresin as approximately 10–11.1% astaxanthin and obtained by supercritical CO₂ extraction. [dr-astaxanthin-s22]

These are food/novel-food safety determinations, not authorized therapeutic claims.

### Sponsorship pattern

Commercial participation is not incidental to this literature. The small UV study evaluated a FUJIFILM product with FUJIFILM-affiliated investigators. [ref-zhou-2021] The formulation-bioavailability trial NCT02397811 was directly sponsored by Cyanotech Corporation. [dr-astaxanthin-s19] A completed exercise/inflammation trial was academically sponsored but listed LycoRed Ltd. as collaborator. [dr-astaxanthin-s23] A 2026 asthma registry study lists AstaReal among collaborators. [dr-astaxanthin-s24]

This does not make the data invalid, but it raises the evidentiary value of independent replication, preregistration, prespecified primary endpoints, complete reporting of null outcomes, and explicit product identification.

### Main unresolved limitations

The literature is dominated by small studies, short follow-up, heterogeneous formulations and doses, proprietary products, numerous surrogate endpoints, multiple-comparison opportunities, and relatively sparse independent replication. Evidence is particularly thin for pregnancy/lactation, renal impairment, hepatic impairment, clinically characterized drug interactions, long-term high-dose exposure, and rare adverse events. The cardiometabolic literature has almost no hard clinical-event evidence, and exercise/inflammation studies repeatedly demonstrate why a favorable biomarker cannot by itself be treated as a clinically meaningful benefit. [dr-astaxanthin-s7][dr-astaxanthin-s8][dr-astaxanthin-s12]

## Mechanisms

```yaml
- title: Formulation-sensitive intestinal absorption and lipoprotein transport
  description: Human pharmacokinetic studies demonstrate that astaxanthin exposure depends strongly on carrier formulation and meal context. After absorption, circulating astaxanthin is distributed among chylomicron/VLDL, LDL and HDL fractions. These pharmacokinetic observations do not themselves demonstrate a clinical benefit.
  sourceId: ref-odeberg-2003
  conceptId: astaxanthin-oral-absorption
- title: Hydrolysis of fatty-acyl astaxanthin esters during absorption
  description: In a three-person human pharmacokinetic study of astaxanthin fatty-acyl diesters, unesterified astaxanthin appeared in plasma while intact esters were not detected, supporting ester hydrolysis before or during systemic uptake. Optical and geometric isomer-selective uptake was also observed.
  sourceId: ref-coral-2004
  conceptId: astaxanthin-oral-absorption
- title: Human oxidative-stress biomarker modulation is context-dependent
  description: Some randomized human studies report lower malondialdehyde or isoprostane and higher total antioxidant capacity, whereas trained-cyclist and exercise-recovery trials report null oxidative-stress outcomes. This supports context-dependent biomarker effects rather than a universal clinical antioxidant outcome.
  sourceId: ref-choi-2011
- title: Exploratory exercise immune-proteome response
  description: In trained runners, 8 mg/day did not modify soreness, CK, conventional cytokines or 42 exercise-responsive oxylipins but was associated with differential recovery of numerous immune-related plasma proteins. This is exploratory molecular evidence and not a validated clinical recovery mechanism.
  sourceId: ref-nieman-2023
```

## Cautions

```yaml
- title: Regulatory food or supplement status is not therapeutic approval
  description: Novel-food or dietary-supplement market pathways address ingredient safety and conditions of use. They do not establish astaxanthin as an approved treatment for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, eye disease, photoaging or exercise-performance enhancement.
  sourceId: ref-eu-2023
- title: High-dose pharmacokinetic studies do not establish chronic high-dose safety
  description: Human single-dose pharmacokinetic studies have used 40-100 mg and one very small exercise study used 28 mg/day for four days. These exposures cannot establish long-term safety at the same doses.
  sourceId: ref-odeberg-2003
- title: Pregnancy and lactation evidence is inadequate
  description: Adequate controlled human pregnancy or lactation safety data were not identified in the reviewed evidence. Lack of a demonstrated adverse signal should not be interpreted as established safety.
  sourceId: ref-efsa-2020
- title: Clinically characterized drug-interaction evidence is sparse
  description: No specific clinically validated astaxanthin drug-drug interaction was identified in the reviewed human evidence. Mechanism-only interaction claims were not promoted to structured interaction records.
  sourceId: ref-efsa-2020
- title: Formulations are not pharmacokinetically interchangeable
  description: Carrier lipid, emulsifier or dispersant, esterification state, geometric and optical isomer distribution, meal timing and dose can materially change exposure. Cross-trial milligram-dose comparisons should therefore preserve formulation context.
  sourceId: ref-odeberg-2003
- title: Biomarkers are not clinical outcomes
  description: Changes in creatine kinase, LDH, malondialdehyde, cytokines, adiponectin, fibrinogen or other biomarkers should not be represented as proof of symptom improvement, disease prevention or improved athletic performance unless corresponding clinical endpoints also improve.
  sourceId: ref-exercise-meta-2026
- title: Pediatric evidence is limited
  description: The identified digital-eye-strain evidence in children is based primarily on one 64-participant randomized trial. It does not establish broad pediatric efficacy or safety across ages and indications.
  sourceId: ref-hecht-2025
```
