## Scope and bottom line

Phenibut is a synthetic GABA analogue used as a prescription medicine in some Eastern-European/post-Soviet settings and sold nonmedically online elsewhere. The central evidentiary problem is that clinical use and marketing claims substantially outrun the quality of the human efficacy literature. WHO's 2021 Expert Committee on Drug Dependence review concluded that no adequately established randomized controlled trial evidence was available for phenibut; the 2020 systematic review it assessed found 11 clinical studies totaling 583 patients, but much of the older literature was open-label, poorly reported, inaccessible in full English translation, or otherwise unsuitable for a robust efficacy estimate. [R1][R5]

Evidence verdict: no indication has high- or moderate-certainty efficacy evidence in the eligible source set. The most recent substantial human study, a 2025 Latvian adjustment-disorder cohort, was uncontrolled and manufacturer-supported; it found symptom improvement but cannot distinguish a drug effect from natural resolution, regression to the mean, expectancy, or other care. [R6]

Healthy-person cognition, anxiety, sociability, and sleep claims are unsupported by adequate human trials. The available treatment studies concern clinical populations rather than healthy volunteers, and a ClinicalTrials.gov search specifically for phenibut as an intervention returned zero records on October 10, 2026. Marketing descriptions such as “nootropic” therefore should not be converted into an efficacy statement. [R4]

Safety is better established as a signal than efficacy. U.S. poison-center surveillance, a regional poison-center cohort, and systematic reviews of toxicity/withdrawal consistently document severe CNS depression, coma or need for airway support in some exposures, alongside agitation, psychosis/delirium, tolerance, dependence, and potentially severe withdrawal. These sources cannot estimate incidence among all users because exposure ascertainment is passive and the systematic reviews are dominated by published severe cases. [R11][R12][R13][R14][R15]

## Therapeutic evidence and studied formulations

The human literature supports recording historical/clinical use, not “proven efficacy.” Exact study doses should likewise be treated as study exposures rather than general dosing recommendations.

| Population/claim | Human evidence and formulation | Evidence interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Adjustment disorder / anxious-neurotic symptoms | In 2025, 90 Latvian adults completed a non-interventional before/after study after receiving Noofen 250 mg hard capsules, 250 mg orally three times daily = 750 mg/day, for at least 3 weeks. ADNM-20 scores fell by a mean 14.8 ± 11.3 points; sleep disturbance, restlessness, and anxiety were among the reported improvements. [R6] | Very low certainty. No untreated/placebo group. JSC Olpha supported the study; two authors were Olpha employees and Olpha manufactures Noofen. [R6] |
| Pediatric tension-type headache | Thirty patients aged 8–16 received Noophen, γ-amino-β-phenylbutyric acid hydrochloride, 15–20 mg/kg/day orally in 2–3 divided doses for 2 months. Headache burden, anxiety, and sleep measures reportedly improved. [R7] | Very low certainty / classification conflict. PubMed tags the article as an RCT, but the accessible abstract does not describe the comparator or randomization method, while WHO later concluded that no phenibut RCT was established. |
| Atypical facial pain with anxiety/depressive symptoms | Thirty of 45 patients received Noophen 500 mg/day for 8 weeks; improvement in psychiatric scales and sleep was reported. [R8] | Very low certainty. No randomized comparator is described in the abstract. |
| Adolescent ADHD | Sixty adolescents were studied; Noophen was evaluated at 500 mg/day for 45 days, with improvement reported in 63.3%. [R9] | Very low certainty. Accessible reporting does not establish blinded, placebo-controlled efficacy. |
| Cognitive impairment with hypertension plus type 2 diabetes | Fifty-six patients were studied using phenibut plus ipidacrine as an adjunct to standard antihypertensive/hypoglycemic therapy; psychometric improvement was reported after one month. [R10] | Cannot isolate phenibut. Multiple active interventions prevent attribution of the effect specifically to phenibut. |
| Chronic fatigue symptoms with chronic cerebrovascular disease | A 2019 report describes 53 patients randomized to standard treatment plus Noofen (n=33) or standard treatment alone (n=20). [R17] | Small, condition-specific comparative study; not evidence of healthy-person enhancement. Its description as randomized is another tension with WHO's later “no RCT” assessment. |

A 2020 systematic review summarized a historical/recommended pharmaceutical daily range of 0.25–2 g/day, but this is not a validated universal dose range and should not be presented as prescribing guidance. The same review found adverse events recorded in 5.66% of subjects in the 11 clinical studies, with somnolence most commonly reported at 1.89%; because those studies were methodologically weak and involved controlled clinical dosing, those percentages should not be generalized to unsupervised products or nonmedical use. [R5]

The salt/formulation distinction matters. The pediatric study explicitly identifies γ-amino-β-phenylbutyric acid hydrochloride; the 2025 Latvian study used Noofen 250 mg hard capsules. FDA also identifies names such as “Phenibut HCl” in the U.S. marketplace. “Phenibut” sold online therefore should not be assumed to have pharmaceutical formulation, dose accuracy, or equivalence to registered Noofen products. [R2][R3][R7]

## Healthy-person claims and pharmacokinetics

No eligible placebo-controlled healthy-volunteer trial was found establishing that phenibut improves memory, attention, executive function, learning, ordinary anxiety, sociability, or sleep in people without a relevant disorder. Studies reporting changes in anxiety, sleep, impulsivity, or cognition involved patients with adjustment disorder, headache, atypical facial pain, ADHD, vascular/metabolic cognitive impairment, or cerebrovascular disease; extrapolating those results to healthy enhancement is unsupported. [R4][R6][R7][R8][R9][R10]

Human pharmacokinetics are inadequately characterized. WHO specifically examined the commonly repeated claims that after a 250 mg oral dose about 65% is excreted unchanged in urine, renal clearance resembles creatinine clearance, and the plasma half-life is approximately 5.3 hours. WHO traced these statements to a secondary phenibut review for which the underlying human primary study was not referenced or independently verifiable. These figures are therefore best recorded as legacy PK claims of uncertain provenance, not established PK constants. [R1]

No eligible primary human PK study was located that adequately establishes oral bioavailability, Tmax, concentration-response relationships, protein binding, metabolic pathways, or a modern mass-balance profile. Likewise, no eligible renal-impairment or hepatic-impairment PK study establishing a dose adjustment was found. The unverified claim of substantial unchanged urinary excretion is insufficient to invent a renal dosing rule. [R1]

Accordingly, the BHWiki record should encode renal impairment: insufficient human evidence and hepatic impairment: insufficient human evidence, rather than asserting either safety or contraindication. The same applies to laboratory hepatotoxicity incidence: the selected evidence does not establish a reliable human rate.

## Dependence, toxicity, and interactions

Phenibut can produce tolerance and physical dependence in the published human safety literature, particularly with repeated daily use at doses well above typical pharmaceutical study exposures. In Feldman et al.'s 2023 systematic review, all 25 included withdrawal publications were case reports or conference abstracts; median pre-withdrawal intake was 10 g/day (IQR 4.75–21.5 g/day; range 1–200 g/day). The shortest exposure preceding reported withdrawal was one week at 2–3 g/day, and withdrawal began as soon as two hours after the last dose in some cases. [R12]

Among those highly selected published withdrawal cases, seizures occurred in 2/25 (8%), intubation in 6/25 (24%), and ICU admission in 11/25 (44%); 64% had worsening severity within the first 24 hours of healthcare contact. Because the dataset is entirely case-based, these figures quantify the published-case series, not the probability that an average phenibut user will experience those outcomes. The authors explicitly noted publication bias, uncertain real-world product dose, possible undisclosed polysubstance exposure, and heterogeneous treatment. [R12]

A separate systematic review of 62 toxicity/withdrawal cases found altered mental status, somnolence, psychosis, and movement disorders common in toxicity reports; 48.7% of the published toxicity cases required intubation. Among withdrawal cases, daily use was reported by 95.7%, with anxiety, irritability/agitation, insomnia, and psychosis prominent. Treatment varied among benzodiazepines, baclofen, barbiturates, antipsychotics, and gabapentinoids; there are no comparative trials establishing an optimal withdrawal regimen. [R11]

Poison-center data provide a less publication-selected, although still passive, safety signal. CDC's analysis of the U.S. National Poison Data System identified 1,320 phenibut exposures from 2009 through 2019. Reported effects included agitation (30.4%), drowsiness/lethargy (29.0%), tachycardia (21.9%), confusion (21.3%), and coma (6.2%); 12.6% were categorized as major effects, and three deaths were reported. Poison-center records do not prove phenibut alone caused each outcome, particularly when coexposures occur. [R13]

A Minnesota poison-center study identified 56 calls from 2000–2018, with more than half showing CNS effects, 10.7% involving withdrawal concerns, documented coingestants in 35.7%, and 11/56 (19.6%) requiring intubation. Twenty-seven cases cited abuse as the reason for use and 13 cited anxiety self-treatment. [R14]

There is useful conflicting safety evidence rather than a uniform dose-toxicity relationship. GIZ-Nord in Germany received 17 phenibut inquiries during 2008–2022; 15 involved reported overdoses of 2–100 g and eight patients were somnolent, yet no respiratory depression or coma was reported in that small series, including the claimed 100-g exposure. That result does not negate the U.S. coma/intubation signal; it demonstrates substantial heterogeneity in exposure verification, co-use, product composition, ascertainment, and individual response. [R15]

Controlled human interaction studies were not found. Therefore the interaction record should distinguish observed co-use and pharmacologic concern from measured drug–drug interaction effects:

| Co-exposure | Human evidence |
|---|---|
| Alcohol | Alcohol was among the most frequently combined substances in the 62-case systematic review. This establishes common co-use in adverse-event cases, not a quantified interaction magnitude. Given phenibut-associated CNS depression, additive impairment is a reasonable safety concern but remains unmeasured in a controlled phenibut–alcohol trial. [R11] |
| Benzodiazepines | Benzodiazepines were also among the most common co-used drugs. Their use in supervised withdrawal management should not be interpreted as evidence that unsupervised co-ingestion is safe. [R11][R12] |
| Opioids | No controlled phenibut–opioid study was found. The GIZ-Nord review specifically warns that co-use with opiates could increase coma/respiratory-depression risk; this is an unquantified safety concern consistent with airway events reported in other phenibut datasets. [R15] |
| Gabapentin/pregabalin | No controlled interaction trial was identified. Mechanistic similarity through α2δ-channel binding is mainly preclinical; the use of gabapentinoids in some withdrawal cases does not establish safety of co-ingestion. [R11] |
| Other sedatives | No quantitative interaction study was found. Phenibut-associated somnolence, depressed consciousness, coma, and airway intervention make additive CNS depression a clinically relevant concern, but the magnitude is unknown. [R14] |

## Special populations, mechanism, and regulatory status

Pregnancy and lactation: no prospective human pregnancy, lactation, placental-transfer, teratogenicity, or developmental-safety study meeting the requested source criteria was found. The systematic withdrawal literature includes a newborn among its cases, showing that neonatal phenibut withdrawal has been reported, but this case-level observation cannot quantify congenital risk or define a safe exposure. Pregnancy/lactation should therefore be coded insufficient human evidence, not “safe” or “contraindicated” on the basis of the present evidence alone. [R12]

Preclinical mechanism, kept separate from therapeutic evidence: WHO's review supports activity at GABA_B receptors and binding to α2δ subunits of voltage-dependent calcium channels, largely from nonhuman/in-vitro evidence. WHO found the often-repeated GABA_A component inadequately substantiated by the accessible literature. Receptor pharmacology supplies mechanistic plausibility, not proof that phenibut improves anxiety, sleep, or cognition in humans. [R1]

United States: FDA states that phenibut does not meet the statutory definition of a dietary ingredient and that products declaring it as such while marketed as dietary supplements are misbranded. FDA issued phenibut-related warning letters in 2019 and has subsequently described enforcement against phenibut-containing products marketed with drug claims. This establishes FDA's food/drug marketing position; it should not be expanded into an unsupported assertion about every aspect of controlled-substance or simple-possession law. [R2][R3]

Latvia: the 2025 Latvian study identifies Noofen as a prescription medicine and cites the Latvian State Agency of Medicines product register; it used registered Noofen 250 mg capsules in routine practice. The live ZVA product endpoint was not independently retrievable in this session, so exact October 2026 authorization details beyond the source-documented product should be marked “not independently reverified.” [R6][R16]

International scheduling: WHO's 2021 ECDD review reported phenibut as not controlled at that time under the 1961 Single Convention, 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances, or 1988 Convention. That is explicitly a 2021 international snapshot, not evidence that all national laws or later scheduling decisions remained unchanged through 2026. [R1]

The principal null/conflicting points to preserve in the BHWiki record are therefore: no adequate healthy-person efficacy evidence; no well-established modern human PK dataset; no controlled interaction studies; no pregnancy, renal-impairment, or hepatic-impairment studies; a PubMed “RCT” classification and a 2019 randomized comparative report that sit uneasily beside WHO's no-established-RCT assessment; and materially different respiratory-severity findings across U.S. and German poison-center datasets. [R1][R7][R11][R14][R15][R17]

## BHWiki extraction rule

Encode therapeutic indications as reported/authorized or investigated, not established efficacy; encode healthy-person cognition/anxiety/sleep as insufficient evidence; encode PK as poorly characterized with unverified legacy half-life/urinary-excretion values; encode dependence/withdrawal and serious CNS toxicity as established safety signals with unknown population incidence; encode alcohol/benzodiazepine/opioid/gabapentinoid interactions as no controlled DDI data, with co-use/additive-depressant safety concern; and keep GABA_B/α2δ pharmacology explicitly under preclinical mechanism, not clinical efficacy. [R1][R6]
