## Scope and evidence verdict

EPA, DHA, and EPA+DHA products are not clinically interchangeable. Neither are triglyceride/re-esterified-triglyceride, ethyl-ester, and free-fatty-acid formulations; prescription products and dietary supplements; or supplements and dietary fish. Trial outcomes depend on molecule, dose, formulation, comparator, baseline cardiovascular risk, statin use, triglyceride level, and endpoint. [R1][R2]

For patient treatment, the clearest clinical-outcome evidence is product-specific: icosapent ethyl, a highly purified EPA ethyl ester, at 4 g/day reduced ischemic cardiovascular events in selected statin-treated high-risk patients with elevated triglycerides in REDUCE-IT. The same conclusion cannot be generalized to “fish oil”: 4 g/day EPA+DHA free-fatty-acid preparation was neutral in STRENGTH, while lower-dose mixed EPA+DHA regimens were largely neutral in VITAL, ASCEND, and OMEMI. [R3][R4][R5][R6][R7]

EPA-only results are not uniform. JELIS found fewer major coronary events with 1.8 g/day EPA plus statin in Japanese patients with hypercholesterolemia; RESPECT-EPA at 1.8 g/day icosapent ethyl produced a numerically favorable but formally nonsignificant primary endpoint, although a secondary coronary composite was significant. A 2021 meta-analysis found larger pooled cardiovascular effects with EPA monotherapy than EPA+DHA, but signals for atrial fibrillation and bleeding with EPA monotherapy. A 2026 formulation-focused meta-analysis concluded that among blinded placebo-controlled cardiovascular-outcome trials, 4 g/day icosapent ethyl remains the only individual formulation independently demonstrated to reduce cardiovascular events. [R8][R9][R10][R2]

For healthy-person enhancement, evidence is weaker. VITAL found no significant primary cardiovascular-event reduction with 1 g/day fish oil (465 mg EPA + 375 mg DHA) in generally healthy older adults. VITAL-DEP found no depression-prevention benefit and a slight increase in its combined depression/clinically relevant-symptom endpoint while longitudinal mood scores were unchanged. Alzheimer disease trials and older prevention trials were generally null overall. Exercise studies suggest possible soreness/recovery effects but not robust generalized performance enhancement or a validated dosing strategy. [R5][R11][R12][R13][R14]

Biomarkers are not clinical outcomes. Omega-3 drugs can lower triglycerides and supplementation can markedly raise circulating EPA/DHA without necessarily reducing MI, stroke, dementia, depression, or mortality. STRENGTH and OMEMI directly demonstrate biochemical exposure without primary cardiovascular benefit. [R15][R7]

### Food effects and formulation

In the ECLIPSE randomized crossover study, 54 overweight adults received single 4-g doses of free-fatty-acid omega-3 and prescription ethyl esters during low- and high-fat conditions. Under low fat, baseline-adjusted EPA+DHA 24-h AUC was approximately fourfold higher with free fatty acid; under high fat, the difference was about 1.3-fold. [R18]

A controlled human study of 72 volunteers taking approximately 3.3 g EPA+DHA/day for two weeks reported bioavailability relative to natural fish oil of about 124% for re-esterified triglycerides, 73% for ethyl esters, and 91% for free fatty acids. A 2025 systematic review similarly placed free fatty acids and re-esterified triglycerides above ethyl esters on average, while emphasizing heterogeneity and lack of proof that acute absorption differences produce chronic clinical superiority. [R22][R23]

For VASCEPA, the U.S. label states that icosapent ethyl is de-esterified during absorption, absorbed via small intestine/lymphatic pathways, reaches peak plasma EPA at about 5 h, and has EPA plasma elimination half-life about 89 h. All trials gave it with/following food; it is labeled with food. [R16]

LOVAZA supplies EPA/DHA as ethyl esters; clinical studies administered it with meals. [R17]

### Prescription products versus supplements

Prescription omega-3 drugs have specified ingredients, doses, indications, contraindications, adverse-event information, and manufacturing controls. FDA states that it does not preapprove dietary supplements for safety/effectiveness or labeling before sale. REDUCE-IT is evidence for icosapent ethyl 4 g/day in the enrolled population, not arbitrary OTC fish oil. [R24]

## Cardiovascular treatment, triglycerides, LDL, AF, and bleeding

**REDUCE-IT:** 8,179 statin-treated high-risk participants; Amarin sponsor. Icosapent ethyl produced the primary five-component ischemic endpoint in 17.2% vs 22.0% placebo, HR 0.75 (95% CI 0.68–0.83) over median 4.9 years. This is product-, dose-, and population-specific evidence. [R3][R25]

**STRENGTH:** AstraZeneca-sponsored, 13,078 high-risk statin-treated patients; 4 g/day EPA+DHA carboxylic acids versus corn oil. Primary MACE occurred in ~12.0% vs 12.2%, HR 0.99 (95% CI 0.90–1.09); stopped for futility. [R4][R19]

**VITAL:** 25,871 generally healthy U.S. adults; 1 g/day fish oil. Primary MACE HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), not significant; MI lower in a secondary analysis but stroke, CV death, and all-cause mortality not significantly reduced. [R5]

**ASCEND:** 15,480 patients with diabetes but no known CVD; 1 g/day omega-3. Serious vascular events rate ratio 0.97 (95% CI 0.87–1.08). [R6]

**OMEMI:** 1,027 adults aged 70–82 after recent MI; 930 mg EPA + 660 mg DHA/day. Primary CV endpoint HR 1.08 (95% CI 0.82–1.41), despite substantial EPA/DHA rises. [R7]

**JELIS:** 18,645 Japanese patients with hypercholesterolemia; 1.8 g EPA plus statin reduced major coronary events 3.5% to 2.8% (19% relative reduction) over mean 4.6 years. Open-label with blinded endpoint assessment; sudden/coronary death not significantly reduced and primary-prevention subgroup did not independently reach significance. [R8]

**RESPECT-EPA:** statin-treated Japanese patients with chronic coronary disease; 1.8 g/day. Primary composite 9.1% vs 12.6%, HR 0.79 (95% CI 0.62–1.00), p=0.055; secondary coronary composite significant, primary result not. [R9]

EMA removed the post-MI secondary-prevention indication for EPA+DHA ethyl-esters after later randomized evidence failed to confirm older open-label benefit; hypertriglyceridemia authorization was unaffected. [R26]

### Triglycerides and LDL

VASCEPA 4 g/day produced placebo-adjusted median triglyceride reduction about 33%, with LDL-C difference −2% (95% CI −13 to +8). [R16]

LOVAZA 4 g/day lowered median triglycerides 44.9% versus 6.7% placebo rise, but LDL-C rose 44.5% from baseline versus a 4.8% placebo fall (between-group median percent difference +49.3 percentage points); label advises LDL monitoring. Triglyceride lowering does not imply CV-event reduction. [R17]

### Atrial fibrillation

A 2021 meta-analysis of seven CV-outcome RCTs (81,210 participants) found AF HR 1.25 (95% CI 1.07–1.46); risk was larger above 1 g/day, with modeled HR about 1.11 per additional g/day. [R27]

In REDUCE-IT/VASCEPA, AF/flutter requiring hospitalization ≥24 h occurred about 3% vs 2%, HR 1.5 (95% CI 1.14–1.98). OMEMI reported new AF 7.2% vs 4.0%, HR 1.84 (95% CI 0.98–3.45); RESPECT-EPA reported 3.1% vs 1.6%, p=0.017. [R16][R7][R9]

EMA pharmacovigilance concluded omega-3-acid ethyl esters have dose-dependent AF risk, highest at 4 g/day, and required label updates; discontinuation is advised if AF develops. [R28][R29]

### Bleeding and anticoagulants

A 2024 meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials (120,643 participants) found no statistically significant overall bleeding increase, RR 1.09 (95% CI 0.91–1.31). High-dose purified EPA subgroup had about 50% relative increase and ~0.6 percentage-point absolute increase. [R30]

VASCEPA labeling reports bleeding about 12% vs 10% and serious bleeding 3% vs 2% in its CV-outcome trial; events were more common with antithrombotics, so monitoring is advised. A small warfarin interaction study found no significant PK/anticoagulation change, which does not cancel the clinical warning. [R16]

LOVAZA labeling notes occasional prolonged bleeding time and advises monitoring with anticoagulants/antiplatelets; postmarketing hemorrhagic diathesis is reported. [R17]

## Cognition, dementia, depression, pregnancy, and infant development

In 402 patients with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer disease, algal DHA 2 g/day for 18 months did not slow ADAS-cog, CDR-sum-of-boxes, or MRI atrophy. [R11] OmegAD (DHA 1.7 g + EPA 0.6 g/day) found no overall cognitive benefit; a very-mild subgroup signal was small and post hoc. [R12]

A Cochrane review of three high-quality prevention RCTs (4,080 randomized; 3,536 final data) found no meaningful MMSE or cognitive benefit over up to about 40 months; incident dementia was not adequately established. [R13] A 2026 network meta-analysis reported domain-specific DHA signals, but mixed-ingredient interventions, small studies, and duration sensitivity prevent a dementia-prevention claim. [R31]

For existing depression, a 2021 Cochrane review of 35 studies found pooled SMD −0.40 (about 2.5 HAM-D17 points), below its cited clinical-importance threshold; certainty was very low and heterogeneity substantial. [R32] Another meta-analysis found SMD −0.28 with stronger EPA-pure/majority subgroup signals, still uncertain. [R33]

VITAL-DEP randomized 18,353 older adults to 1 g/day fish oil versus placebo for median 5.3 years. Depression/clinically relevant symptoms were slightly more frequent with omega-3, HR 1.13 (95% CI 1.01–1.26), while longitudinal PHQ-8 scores did not differ. [R11]

The 2018 Cochrane pregnancy review (70 RCTs; 19,927 women) found reduced preterm birth <37 weeks (RR 0.89) and early preterm birth <34 weeks (RR 0.58), but longer gestation and low/very-low certainty for long-term child outcomes. [R34] The large ORIP RCT (5,517 pregnancies; 900 mg/day) found early preterm birth 2.2% vs 2.0%, adjusted RR 1.13 (95% CI 0.79–1.63), null overall; very-large-for-gestational-age births increased. [R21]

DOMInO (2,399 women; 800 mg DHA/day) found high maternal depressive symptoms 9.67% vs 11.19%, adjusted RR 0.85 (95% CI 0.70–1.02), p=0.09, and no 18-month cognitive/language benefit. [R20] A 2024 DHA meta-analysis found higher average birth weight and lower low-birth-weight risk at 450–800 mg/day, but no statistically significant pooled preterm-birth effect and high heterogeneity. [R35]

FDA/EPA recommends 8–12 oz/week lower-mercury seafood during pregnancy/breastfeeding, recognizing fish also supplies protein, iodine, iron, choline, selenium, and other nutrients. Fish is a distinct exposure from capsules. [R36]

## Exercise, inflammation, dietary fish, and healthy-person enhancement

A 2026 review/meta-analysis of 43 exercise studies (9 placebo-controlled eccentric-exercise trials in the quantitative synthesis) found less DOMS, lower CK, less swelling, and modestly better strength/range of motion at peak impairment, but judged the literature equivocal and methodologically limited. [R14] Earlier review of 15 RCTs/461 healthy participants found no consistent improvement in hypertrophy, strength, inflammatory response, or muscle-damage outcomes beyond training, although some recovery signals existed. [R37]

EPA and DHA had broadly similar effects on CRP, IL-6, TNF-α and adiponectin in a biomarker meta-analysis; biomarkers are not clinical disease or longevity outcomes. [R38]

Prospective cohorts associate fatty-fish intake (not uniformly lean fish) with lower CHD incidence/mortality and total mortality, but CVD outcomes are inconsistent and confounding/substitution/species/preparation differences remain. Observational fish intake cannot establish capsule efficacy. [R39][R40][R41]

Evidence hierarchy for a healthy adult: dietary fish in a healthy food pattern ≠ low-dose OTC fish oil ≠ pharmacologic prescription omega-3 therapy.

## Safety, contaminants, and regulatory interpretation

Established high-dose signals are AF, modest absolute bleeding risk with high-dose EPA/antithrombotics, and formulation-specific LDL increases with DHA-containing EPA+DHA ethyl esters. [R27][R28][R30][R16][R17]

FDA/EPA fish advice is the strongest eligible contaminant-related regulatory evidence here: choose lower-mercury species, especially in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and childhood, and observe local advisories for locally caught fish. No market-wide claim that supplements are free of mercury, PCBs, oxidation products, or label inaccuracies is made; product-assay literature was outside the primary-human/regulatory scope used for this report. [R36]

FDA does not preapprove dietary supplements for safety/effectiveness before sale. Prescription products have specified active ingredients, dose, indications, contraindications, and manufacturing controls. An OTC “1,000 mg fish oil” label is not evidence of the dose, chemical form, PK, purity, or CV efficacy of a prescription product. [R24]

| Preventing cognitive decline/dementia | Not established; older randomized prevention evidence null. [R13][R31] |
| Treating established Alzheimer disease | No convincing benefit from pivotal DHA or DHA+EPA trials. [R11][R12] |
| Treating depression | Possible small effect, low/very-low certainty; EPA-dominant subgroup signals uncertain. [R32][R33] |

## Evidence limitations and BHWiki takeaways

The central problem is heterogeneity disguised by “omega-3.” REDUCE-IT tested prescription EPA ethyl ester 4 g/day in statin-treated high-risk patients; STRENGTH tested 4 g/day free-fatty-acid EPA+DHA; VITAL tested 840 mg/day combined EPA+DHA in largely healthy primary prevention. These are different interventions, not one uniform treatment. [R3][R4][R5]

The same applies to LDL effects: EPA-only icosapent ethyl lowered triglycerides without the marked LDL rise seen with DHA-containing EPA+DHA ethyl esters. Absorption superiority of free fatty acids/re-esterified triglycerides over ethyl esters cannot be converted into a blanket claim of superior cardiovascular, cognitive, mood, or exercise outcomes. [R16][R17][R18][R22]

**Most defensible BHWiki summary:** prescription omega-3 therapy has strong product-specific triglyceride evidence and high-quality CV benefit for icosapent ethyl 4 g/day in selected statin-treated high-risk patients; AF and bleeding risks must be included, and DHA-containing products can raise LDL. Healthy-person supplementation has no convincing routine prevention evidence for major CV events, dementia, or depression; exercise recovery effects are possible but not broadly ergogenic. Pregnancy effects are mixed; routine infant-IQ claims are unsupported. Fish is a distinct, regulator-supported dietary exposure when lower-mercury species are chosen. Biomarkers must not be presented as clinical outcomes.
