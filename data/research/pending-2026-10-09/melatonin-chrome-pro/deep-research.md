## Evidence scope and entity distinctions

Melatonin should not be treated as one interchangeable intervention. Endogenous melatonin is a darkness-associated circadian signal: night-time light can suppress endogenous secretion, and measured dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) or melatonin/6-sulfatoxymelatonin concentrations are biomarkers of timing/exposure, not clinical benefits by themselves. The evidence below therefore keeps four entities separate:

| Entity | BHWiki treatment |
|---|---|
| Endogenous melatonin | Circadian biomarker/physiology. DLMO, plasma melatonin, and 6-sulfatoxymelatonin should be reported separately from sleep, function, symptoms, or quality of life. |
| Immediate-release oral melatonin | Short, formulation-dependent exposure; useful evidence exists for phase shifting, DSWPD, and jet lag, but fast-release melatonin is not recommended as routine chronic-insomnia pharmacotherapy in current European guidance. [R13] |
| Sublingual/buccal/transdermal melatonin | Pharmacokinetically distinct and comparatively under-studied. Demonstrating higher or prolonged plasma exposure does not establish better patient-important sleep outcomes. |
| Prolonged-release/prescription products | Must be linked to the exact product, indication, age group, dose and regulator. EU prescription products such as Circadin and Slenyto should not be conflated with U.S. dietary supplements. Current Slenyto instructions and warnings are formulation-specific. [R29] |

Mechanistic receptor pharmacology, animal findings, and in-vitro antioxidant/immunologic findings were excluded from efficacy conclusions. Human biomarker effects are retained but explicitly labeled as biomarkers.

## Pharmacokinetics, circadian timing, and formulation

### Immediate-release oral exposure

The existing BHWiki statement that immediate-release melatonin has an approximately 40–60-minute elimination half-life is defensible if presented as a formulation- and study-dependent approximation rather than a universal constant. Human studies report approximately 36–41 minutes at a 250-µg oral/IV tracer dose, about 42.6 ± 12.6 minutes after 0.5 mg in the PK study cited by the DSWPD investigators, and 53.7 ± 7.0 minutes after 10 mg oral melatonin in another crossover PK trial. The broader systematic-review prototype is roughly Tmax 30–60 minutes and t½ around 45–55 minutes for conventional immediate-release oral formulations. [R1–R4] Sletten et al. specifically cited a 0.5-mg Tmax of about 30 minutes and t½ 42.6 ± 12.6 minutes when selecting their DSWPD regimen.

Absolute oral bioavailability is low and very variable, not reliably “15%” for every product or person. DeMuro et al. estimated approximately 15% after 2–4 mg oral versus IV melatonin; Fourtillan et al. found individual values from approximately 1% to 37% with sex-group means around 9% and 17%; Andersen et al. found a median 2.5% after 10 mg oral versus 10 mg IV in healthy men. [R2–R4] The variability is clinically important when comparing supplement milligram doses: administered dose does not predict plasma exposure linearly across formulations or people.

### Sublingual and prolonged-release kinetics

A 2023 randomized crossover PK study in healthy men compared a 1-mg immediate-release sublingual spray with a 1.9-mg prolonged-release oral tablet. The spray produced Cmax about 2,332–2,380 pg/mL, Tmax 23.3 ± 6.5 min, and t½ 47.5 ± 19.6 min; the PR tablet produced Cmax 1,151 ± 565 pg/mL, Tmax 64.2 ± 44.2 min, and t½ 133.3 ± 43.1 min. Dose-adjusted exposure from the spray was approximately 1.89 times the PR tablet. Plasma melatonin/6-sulfatoxymelatonin AUC ratios were 0.16 versus 0.09. [R5]

That study did not contain an IV comparator, so its “bioavailability” comparison is relative, not absolute bioavailability. It was conducted during daytime in healthy volunteers and measured pharmacokinetics, not insomnia or functional outcomes; therefore its higher/faster sublingual peak cannot be converted into a claim of superior clinical efficacy. The study products were marketed by PiLeJe Laboratoire and the study was industry funded, increasing the importance of separating PK findings from authors' therapeutic extrapolations. [R5]

Human transdermal/buccal literature is much thinner. Systematic reviews describe highly formulation-dependent absorption and, for transdermal preparations, potentially slow/depot-like exposure, but found insufficient formulation-to-outcome evidence to recommend transdermal, buccal, or any particular PK profile over conventional oral products for a specific sleep disorder. [R6] An ex-vivo human-skin/porcine-buccal permeation paper sometimes cited for transdermal melatonin is not a clinical human trial and therefore does not support clinical efficacy here.

### Circadian dose timing and light

Timing can matter as much as dose. In a controlled human phase-response study, 0.5 mg melatonin produced its largest phase advances when given approximately 2–4 hours before baseline DLMO; the optimal timing for 0.5 mg was later than for a 3-mg dose, while optimally timed maximal phase shifts were of similar magnitude. This is a DLMO phase-shift result, not proof that taking melatonin 2–4 hours before DLMO maximizes patient-reported sleep benefit in every DSWPD patient. [R7]

The clinically important 2018 DSWPD trial deliberately used 0.5 mg fast-release melatonin 1 hour before the patient's desired bedtime, together with a fixed behavioral sleep-wake schedule. It advanced actigraphic sleep onset by 34 minutes versus placebo, shortened actigraphic sleep-onset latency by about 12 minutes and subjective latency by about 18 minutes, improved early-night sleep efficiency and several impairment/symptom scores, yet did not significantly shift post-treatment DLMO in the measured subset. That discordance is important: the demonstrated clinical benefit was not contingent on showing a biomarker phase advance. [R9]

Because endogenous secretion responds to darkness and light at night suppresses it, light environment is part of circadian treatment rather than an incidental detail. Exogenous melatonin should therefore not be represented as a substitute for appropriately timed light/dark exposure or behavioral scheduling in a circadian disorder.

## Efficacy by indication and population

### Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder

The strongest directly applicable pragmatic trial remains Sletten et al.: 0.5 mg fast-release 1 hour before desired bedtime, at least five consecutive nights per week for four weeks, plus attempting sleep at the desired bedtime. Among 116 randomized patients with DSWPD and DLMO delayed relative to their desired bedtime, sleep began 34 minutes earlier by actigraphy versus placebo; sleep-related impairment, sleep disturbance, insomnia severity and disability also improved, and 52.8% versus 24.0% had more than minimal clinician-rated improvement. DLMO itself did not significantly differ after treatment in the measured subset. Light-headedness, daytime sleepiness and decreased libido occurred but were not significantly more frequent than placebo. [R9]

AASM's circadian-rhythm guideline gives strategically timed melatonin only a weak recommendation for adults with DSWPD because the evidence base is limited and heterogeneous. Thus the BHWiki record should preserve the 0.5-mg trial, but not translate it into “0.5 mg is universally optimal” or assume that larger doses are better. [R8]

### Jet lag and shift work

The classic Cochrane jet-lag review found that melatonin taken near the destination bedtime, roughly 22:00–24:00, reduced jet-lag symptoms in most included randomized trials, particularly after crossing five or more time zones. 0.5–5 mg produced similar overall jet-lag effects; 5 mg tended to improve sleep onset/quality more than 0.5 mg, doses above 5 mg showed no additional benefit, and a 2-mg slow-release preparation was relatively ineffective. Incorrect daytime timing can cause sleepiness and can shift the circadian system in the wrong direction. The evidence is old and based on small heterogeneous travel studies, so unusually favorable effect estimates should not be treated as modern high-certainty evidence. [R10]

For shift workers, a Cochrane review of nine melatonin trials found 1–10 mg after a night shift increased subsequent daytime sleep by about 24 minutes (95% CI 9.8–38.9) and night-time sleep by about 17 minutes, but did not materially improve sleep latency; evidence quality was low. This does not establish improved alertness, accident reduction, cognition, or performance during the next shift. NCCIH likewise characterizes shift-work findings as small or inconclusive. [R11,R34]

### Primary and secondary insomnia

For general chronic insomnia in adults, evidence is much weaker than popular supplement use implies. The AASM pharmacologic guideline suggests not using melatonin for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults; CBT-I remains the preferred first-line treatment. NCCIH likewise concludes that the evidence is not strong enough to recommend supplementation for chronic insomnia. [R12,R34]

Formulation is important. The 2023 European insomnia guideline allows prolonged-release melatonin for up to three months in patients ≥55 years, while specifically not recommending fast-release melatonin for insomnia. A 2023 meta-analysis found PR melatonin versus placebo shortened subjective sleep latency by only 6.3 minutes, objective latency by 5.05 minutes, and increased objective sleep efficiency by 1.91 percentage points; effects were generally small, though some older-adult analyses were more favorable. [R13,R15]

EMA-authorized Circadin is a 2-mg prolonged-release prescription product for short-term treatment of primary insomnia characterized by poor sleep quality in patients aged 55 years or older; the labeled regimen is 2 mg once daily after food, 1–2 hours before bedtime, for up to 13 weeks. [R14]

For secondary insomnia, evidence is condition-specific and should not be pooled into a general “melatonin treats comorbid insomnia” claim. In adults with cancer, a 2024 systematic review of 61 randomized trials found CBT-I had the clearest evidence; melatonin and several other interventions showed possible benefits but evidence was less convincing.

### Children and neurodevelopmental populations

A manufacturer-sponsored phase III trial enrolled 125 children/adolescents aged 2–17.5 years, almost all with ASD, after behavioral sleep intervention had failed. Pediatric PR melatonin began at 2 mg nightly, with escalation to 5 mg if necessary. At 13 weeks, sleep duration increased by 57.5 minutes from baseline versus 9.1 minutes on placebo, sleep latency decreased by 39.6 versus 12.5 minutes, and 68.9% versus 39.3% met the trial's clinically meaningful sleep response definition; somnolence was more frequent. The trial registry confirms Neurim Pharmaceuticals Ltd. as lead sponsor. [R16]

The extension followed participants on 2, 5 or 10 mg nightly for up to 104 weeks. Sleep/caregiver improvements persisted, while fatigue and somnolence were the most frequent treatment-related adverse events. Recorded height, weight, BMI and Tanner-stage development did not show delayed growth or puberty. However, the long-term phase was predominantly open-label and only 31 participants aged ≥8 had usable Tanner data at the two-year assessment, so this is reassuring rather than definitive evidence that long-term childhood exposure has no endocrine developmental effects. [R17]

Current EU Slenyto labeling is more specific than the supplement literature. For insomnia in children/adolescents aged 2–18 with ASD and/or relevant neurogenetic disorders, starting dose is 2 mg once daily, titrated as required up to 10 mg; for ADHD-associated insomnia aged 6–17, starting dose is 1–2 mg, with individualized titration to 5 mg and, if necessary, 10 mg. It is taken 30–60 minutes before bedtime after the evening meal and treatment should be regularly reassessed. These prescription instructions must not be copied onto unregulated immediate-release supplements. [R29]

## Older adults, cognition, depression, anxiety, and pain

Older adults may have longer persistence of exogenous melatonin and more daytime drowsiness. Evidence supporting PR melatonin in ≥55-year-olds is therefore product/formulation-specific rather than an endorsement of arbitrary high-dose immediate-release supplements.

A meta-analysis of randomized cognitive studies found that daytime melatonin in healthy adults reduced response accuracy (SMD −0.74) while pooled reaction time and memory measures were not consistently impaired. Apparent MMSE improvements in some Alzheimer-disease trials were population-specific and do not establish general cognitive enhancement or disease modification. [R20]

For depression, a 2022 meta-analysis of 19 studies/1,178 participants using 2–25 mg/day found no statistically significant overall improvement in depressive symptoms (SMD −0.17, 95% CI −0.38 to 0.05), despite a favorable BDI-only subgroup. Melatonin therefore should not be represented as an established antidepressant. [R21]

For anxiety, evidence is narrow: systematic reviews support a reduction in preoperative anxiety, with less clear postoperative benefit and no basis for extrapolation to generalized anxiety disorders. [R22]

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of 23 randomized trials and 2,028 participants found only modest, uncertain analgesic effects. In chronic musculoskeletal pain, the overall placebo comparison was not statistically significant (−6.76/100, 95% CI −15.89 to 2.36), though a low-risk-of-bias sensitivity analysis was positive; most estimated effects did not reach minimal clinically important difference thresholds, and certainty was low to moderate. Melatonin is therefore, at most, a possible adjunct—not an established primary analgesic. [R23]

## Safety and special populations

### Adverse effects and next-day impairment

Across 37 randomized sleep-disorder trials using 0.15–12 mg/day, the most commonly reported adverse events were daytime sleepiness (1.66%), headache (0.74%), dizziness (0.74%), other sleep-related adverse effects (0.74%) and hypothermia (0.62%). Most trials were short and adverse-event reporting was often weak, limiting inference about chronic use. [R18]

A systematic review of doses ≥10 mg identified 79 studies but found that 29 did not even state whether adverse events occurred; only four studies qualified for its lower-risk meta-analysis. In those studies, serious adverse events and AE withdrawals were not detectably increased, but overall adverse effects such as drowsiness, headache and dizziness were increased (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.15–1.69). This is not evidence that megadoses are proven safe long-term. [R19]

Prescription PR labeling also recognizes clinically relevant residual effects. Current Slenyto information lists drowsiness, tiredness and a “hangover feeling” among common adverse effects and tells patients not to drive, cycle, or operate machinery until recovered. Thus “melatonin causes no next-day impairment” is too broad; impairment depends on dose, timing, formulation, metabolism and measurement. [R29]

### Seizure and epilepsy considerations

The two-year pediatric PedPRM extension included participants with pre-existing epilepsy and did not identify a clear treatment-related increase in seizures; observed seizures were judged unlikely to be related to study drug. The trial was not powered to establish seizure safety, however. Official NIH/NCCIH guidance still advises that people with epilepsy use melatonin under medical supervision. No anticonvulsant clinical benefit should be inferred from receptor or animal literature. [R17,R34]

### Pregnancy, lactation, renal and hepatic impairment

Human pregnancy and lactation safety evidence is inadequate. NCCIH describes a lack of research in pregnant or breastfeeding populations. Current EU Slenyto information advises avoiding melatonin during pregnancy as a precaution and notes that melatonin may pass into human breast milk, requiring an individualized breastfeeding decision. [R29,R34]

For Slenyto, renal-impairment pharmacokinetics have not been adequately studied and the regulator advises caution; experience in hepatic impairment is lacking and use is not recommended in the product information. These label restrictions are more defensible than extrapolating normal-volunteer PK to severe renal or hepatic disease. [R29]

### Pediatric poisoning and long-term uncertainty

U.S. poison-center reports involving people ≤19 years rose from 8,337 in 2012 to 52,563 in 2021. Most exposures were accidental and asymptomatic, but 4,097 hospitalizations, 287 ICU admissions and five mechanical-ventilation cases were recorded; two deaths in children <2 were reported without evidence establishing melatonin as the cause. A separate CDC analysis estimated approximately 11,000 emergency-department visits for unsupervised ingestion among children ≤5 during 2019–2022. These pharmacovigilance data primarily establish a storage/exposure hazard, not toxicity at therapeutic doses. [R34]

## Interactions and cardiometabolic effects

### CYP1A2, CYP3A, caffeine, smoking and contraceptives

The clearest clinically relevant pathway in human and regulatory evidence is CYP1A-mediated metabolism, particularly CYP1A2. Current EU product information does not establish a CYP3A-based dose adjustment; therefore a clinically important CYP3A interaction should not be asserted solely from in-vitro metabolism experiments. [R29]

Fluvoxamine is the strongest documented interaction in the prescription label: coadministration increased melatonin AUC approximately 17-fold and Cmax approximately 12-fold, attributed to CYP1A2/CYP2C19 inhibition; coadministration is to be avoided. [R29]

Ciprofloxacin and other quinolone CYP1A2 inhibitors can increase melatonin exposure, while carbamazepine and rifampicin can lower concentrations by enzyme induction; the EU label advises caution/dose reassessment rather than providing a universal quantitative adjustment. [R29]

Caffeine can substantially increase exposure. In a human crossover interaction study using 6 mg melatonin and caffeine 200 mg three times, caffeine increased mean melatonin Cmax by about 142% and AUC by about 120%, with variability related to smoking/genotype. [R28]

Smoking can increase CYP1A2 activity. In eight habitual smokers given a very high experimental 25-mg oral melatonin dose, exogenous melatonin AUC was substantially lower while actively smoking than after seven days' abstinence; endogenous overnight melatonin exposure was not comparably altered. This is another reason to distinguish endogenous biomarkers from exogenous-drug PK. [R31]

Hormonal contraception/oestrogens can markedly increase exposure. A human 6-mg PK study found melatonin AUC and Cmax approximately four- to five-fold higher in oral-contraceptive users than nonusers; current EU labeling also warns that oestrogens can increase melatonin concentrations through CYP1A inhibition. [R29,R32]

Alcohol should not be assumed to be a benign combination: current EU labeling says alcohol can reduce melatonin's effect on sleep and advises avoidance around treatment. [R29]

For warfarin/other anticoagulants, robust quantified randomized DDI evidence is lacking in the included source set. The older jet-lag review noted case-report concern, while current NIH guidance conservatively recommends medical supervision for people taking blood-thinner medicines. This supports a precaution, not a claim that melatonin has been proven to cause major bleeding. [R10,R34]

### Blood pressure

Claims that melatonin is an antihypertensive are not supported by patient-important cardiovascular outcomes. A 2022 meta-analysis found only four hypertension RCTs and no trial reporting cardiovascular events. Controlled-release melatonin produced an estimated −3.57 mm Hg asleep systolic BP difference, but its 95% CI crossed zero (−7.88 to +0.73); awake/asleep diastolic differences were also not statistically significant. Evidence quality was low to very low. Earlier small PR trials were more positive, accounting for part of the apparent conflict in this literature; immediate- and controlled-release preparations should not be pooled indiscriminately. [R27]

### Glucose and metabolic claims

Acute melatonin can worsen glucose handling when given close to food. In 21 healthy women, 5 mg 15 minutes before a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test increased glucose AUC by 186% in the morning and 54% in the evening relative to placebo; glucose Cmax rose 21% and 27%, respectively. Morning impairment was associated mainly with lower insulin release and evening impairment with lower insulin sensitivity. This result does not prove that customary bedtime melatonin causes diabetes, but it does argue against representing melatonin as uniformly metabolically beneficial and makes dose-to-meal timing clinically relevant. [R26]

### Immune, inflammatory and antioxidant claims

Human trials provide evidence for changes in some biomarkers, not for broad “immune boosting.” A meta-analysis of 31 clinical trials/1,517 participants reported reductions in IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8, but no significant CRP reduction; the apparent TNF effect became nonsignificant after removal of outlier studies. None of those biomarker changes alone demonstrates fewer infections, better autoimmune-disease outcomes, lower mortality or other patient-important immune benefits. [R24]

Likewise, meta-analyses have reported changes in oxidative-stress markers such as malondialdehyde, protein carbonyls and total antioxidant capacity, while emphasizing small heterogeneous studies and the need for confirmation. A 2025 randomized trial in diabetic CKD patients, for example, found no statistically significant improvement in its measured oxidative/inflammatory biomarkers after treatment, illustrating that “antioxidant” biochemical plausibility does not guarantee a reproducible human effect. No general anti-aging, cardioprotective or disease-prevention claim should be inferred from these laboratory endpoints. [R25]

## Regulation, quality, sponsorship, and evidence interpretation

In the United States, melatonin is marketed as a dietary supplement, with less stringent premarket regulation than prescription or OTC drugs. This regulatory category is fundamentally different from EMA-authorized prescription melatonin formulations. [R34]

Product-content variability is clinically meaningful. A 2017 analysis summarized by NCCIH found substantial discordance between labeled and measured melatonin content and detected serotonin in 26% of tested supplements. A 2023 U.S. gummy analysis found 22 of 25 products inaccurately labeled; among products containing melatonin, measured amounts ranged from 74% to 347% of label claim. Consequently, milligram doses from trials using standardized pharmaceutical preparations should not automatically be assumed equivalent to arbitrary commercial supplements. [R34]

In Europe, Circadin and Slenyto are regulated medicinal products with specific release profiles, indications, manufacturing standards and dosing instructions. Current Slenyto labeling specifies whole-tablet administration to preserve prolonged release and gives population-specific dosing rather than generic “melatonin” dosing. [R14,R29]

The evidence base shows a clear sponsorship pattern worth preserving in BHWiki. The pivotal pediatric PR trial and its extension arise from a trial whose registered lead sponsor was Neurim Pharmaceuticals Ltd., the product developer. The sublingual-spray/PR pharmacokinetic comparison evaluated marketed PiLeJe products and was PiLeJe-funded. In contrast, the important 0.5-mg DSWPD trial was principally publicly funded, although investigators disclosed some commercial/device-company relationships unrelated or only indirectly related to melatonin. Industry sponsorship does not invalidate a result, but it should be visible beside effect sizes—particularly where a proprietary prolonged-release formulation is being generalized to “melatonin.” [R5,R9,R16,R17,R33]

## BHWiki bottom line

**Supported with important qualifications:** strategically timed low-dose immediate-release melatonin can help selected DSWPD patients; melatonin near destination bedtime can reduce jet lag; PR prescription products have modest insomnia efficacy in specific older-adult and pediatric neurodevelopmental populations; adverse effects are usually mild in short trials.

**Not established or commonly overstated:** a universal optimal melatonin dose; equivalence of IR, PR, sublingual and transdermal products; routine efficacy for general adult chronic insomnia; antidepressant or generalized anxiolytic efficacy; clinically important cognitive enhancement; broad analgesic efficacy; cardiovascular prevention; metabolic improvement; immune enhancement; antioxidant-mediated disease prevention; or long-term safety of arbitrary high-dose supplementation.

The most important record-level correction is therefore: do not describe melatonin by milligram dose alone. Record formulation, release profile, dose, clock/circadian timing, light context, population, and whether the reported endpoint is a biomarker/sleep-timing measure or a patient-important outcome.
