## Summary

L-tyrosine is the free amino-acid form of tyrosine and a precursor for catecholamines, thyroid hormones, melanin, and proteins.[R14] In randomized human studies, acute free L-tyrosine most consistently preserved selected working-memory, vigilance, and information-processing tasks during cold exposure or sleep loss; it is not a reliably effective general cognitive enhancer in rested conditions.[R1][R2][R3][R5][R6] A later randomized trial found worse cognitive flexibility after tyrosine under high cognitive load.[R9]

N-acetyl-L-tyrosine (NALT) is an acetylated derivative, not the same compound as free L-tyrosine. The available human NALT study measured intravenous precursor handling rather than cognition and found substantial unchanged urinary excretion, so superior oral delivery or cognitive equivalence is not established.[R13]

## Description

Free L-tyrosine is incorporated into proteins and can be produced from L-phenylalanine.[R14] Tyrosine hydroxylase converts it to L-DOPA; downstream enzymes produce dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine.[R14] Tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin participate in formation of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), while tyrosinase-dependent pathways produce melanin.[R14]

Most human cognition trials used free L-tyrosine, usually as a single oral load. The evidence below should not be transferred automatically to NALT.[R2][R3][R4][R5][R6][R8][R9]

## Evidence note

A systematic review of 15 placebo-controlled studies in healthy adults found that positive cognitive findings were concentrated in demanding conditions such as cold, heat, multitasking, or sleep loss; most studies were acute, small, and heterogeneous.[R2] Another review concluded that benefits are most plausible when catecholamine function is temporarily challenged or depleted, rather than when baseline neurotransmitter function is intact.[R1]

The evidence is therefore strongest for task-specific, short-term preservation of performance under acute stress. It is weaker for rested-state enhancement, long-term supplementation, disease treatment, mood treatment, or athletic performance.[R1][R2]

## Doses

The following are research exposures, not a general-use dosing protocol.

| Context | Human exposure | Main result |
|---|---:|---|
| Plasma pharmacokinetics | 100 or 150 mg/kg orally, fasting | Plasma tyrosine peaked at approximately 2 hours and remained elevated for 2–8 hours.[R11] |
| Cold chamber | 150 mg/kg orally, 2 hours before testing | Reversed a cold-induced working-memory deficit; no effect occurred at 22°C.[R3] |
| Cold-water immersion | 150 mg/kg before each of two immersions; total 300 mg/kg | Improved selected match-to-sample and reaction-time outcomes during approximately 10°C immersion.[R4] |
| Extended wakefulness | 150 mg/kg split dose after approximately 6 hours of testing | Reduced deterioration in psychomotor performance and high-event-rate vigilance for about 3 hours.[R5] |
| Sleep deprivation | 150 mg/kg after overnight sleep deprivation | Improved several, but not all, cognitive and motor measures; effects were less broad than with d-amphetamine.[R6] |
| Heat study | Two 75-mg/kg or two 150-mg/kg pharmacokinetic doses; separate military heat crossover | Serum tyrosine rose substantially, but cognition and physical performance did not improve.[R8] |
| Cognitive-load study | 2.0 g orally | Worsened cognitive flexibility after high cognitive load in a 70-person randomized trial.[R9] |
| Short-term safety study | 1, 2, 3, or 4 g/day for 4 weeks | No serious adverse events or clinically meaningful dose-related laboratory abnormalities in healthy adult men under study conditions.[R14] |
| NALT precursor study | 5 g N-acetyl-L-tyrosine by intravenous infusion over 4 hours | Only a modest plasma-tyrosine rise occurred; 56% of the infused NALT was excreted unchanged within 4 hours.[R13] |

## Pharmacokinetics

In 12 fasting adults, 100 or 150 mg/kg of oral free L-tyrosine produced maximal plasma concentrations at approximately 2 hours; concentrations remained significantly elevated for up to 8 hours.[R11] The plasma tyrosine ratio relative to competing neutral amino acids increased from approximately 0.10 at baseline to 0.28 after 100 mg/kg and 0.35 after 150 mg/kg.[R11]

In older adults, 100- and 150-mg/kg doses peaked around 90 minutes, while 200 mg/kg peaked around 120 minutes; concentrations remained above baseline at 4 hours.[R10] Older adults had a larger plasma response than younger adults given the same 150-mg/kg dose, and higher doses were associated with poorer load-dependent working-memory performance in that study.[R10]

Tyrosine and other large neutral amino acids share transport processes relevant to brain entry. In a fed human study, oral tyrosine increased the plasma tyrosine ratio despite a diet containing 113 g of protein; the intervention did not materially change the concentrations of the other measured competing neutral amino acids.[R12]

NALT pharmacokinetics are less established. During 5-g intravenous infusions in 11 healthy volunteers, plasma NALT rose rapidly, plasma free tyrosine increased by approximately 25%, and 56% of administered NALT appeared in urine unchanged within 4 hours; no net splanchnic production of tyrosine was detected.[R13] Because this was intravenous rather than oral administration, it does not fully define oral NALT absorption, but it does not support a claim of clearly superior conversion to free tyrosine.[R13]

## Modifiers

- **Stressor type:** Positive cognitive findings occurred in cold exposure and sleep-loss paradigms, whereas a military heat-exercise study found no cognitive or performance benefit despite marked serum tyrosine elevation.[R3][R4][R5][R6][R8]
- **Baseline state:** The cold-chamber study found benefit at 4°C but no effect at 22°C, supporting a state-dependent rather than universal effect.[R3]
- **Cognitive demand:** A 2-g dose worsened cognitive flexibility after a demanding memory task in a later randomized trial, showing that “stress” and cognitive load do not produce identical responses.[R9]
- **Age and dose:** Older adults had greater plasma exposure and poorer working-memory performance at higher doses in one dose-ranging trial.[R10]
- **Timing:** Cognitive studies commonly tested participants about 1–2 hours after ingestion, overlapping with the observed plasma peak.[R3][R5][R11]
- **Food and competing amino acids:** Protein context changes the relative availability of neutral amino acids relevant to brain transport, although the human data do not define a universal fed-versus-fasted rule.[R11][R12]
- **Chemical form:** The cognition literature is primarily free L-tyrosine; NALT has different pharmacokinetic evidence and should not be treated as an interchangeable evidence base.[R13]

## Effects

| Domain | Human randomized findings |
|---|---|
| **Cold and working memory** | In eight men, 150 mg/kg restored performance at the longest delayed-matching interval impaired by 4°C exposure; the same dose did not improve performance at 22°C.[R3] In 19 participants undergoing repeated cold-water immersion, two 150-mg/kg doses improved selected match-to-sample accuracy and processing-speed measures relative to cold placebo.[R4] |
| **Sleep loss and vigilance** | During more than 24 hours of wakefulness, a 150-mg/kg split dose reduced deterioration in psychomotor performance and vigilance for roughly 3 hours.[R5] In a larger 76-man study after sleep deprivation, tyrosine improved several measures of running memory, reasoning, mathematical processing, tracking, and vigilance, but not every task.[R6] |
| **Sleep and recovery** | In a separate 76-man sleep-deprivation protocol, 150 mg/kg did not change sleep quantity, sleep quality, or recovery-sleep architecture, although it stimulated prolactin release.[R7] |
| **Heat stress** | In a small military heat-exercise crossover, tyrosine did not improve cognition, physiology, or time-trial performance despite marked serum tyrosine increases.[R8] |
| **Rested or thermoneutral cognition** | No benefit was found in the thermoneutral 22°C condition of the cold-chamber study.[R3] The broader systematic-review literature does not support a consistent, generalized rested-state enhancement effect.[R1][R2] |
| **Cognitive flexibility** | In 70 healthy adults, 2 g of tyrosine worsened Wisconsin Card Sorting Test flexibility after high cognitive load rather than preventing impairment.[R9] |

## Outcomes

- **Most defensible outcome:** acute preservation of selected cognitive performance during cold exposure or sustained wakefulness, especially working memory, vigilance, and information processing.[R1][R3][R4][R5][R6]
- **Not established:** a broad rested-state “focus” effect, reliable mood enhancement, sleep replacement, or treatment of a psychiatric or neurological disorder.[R1][R2][R7]
- **Physical performance:** evidence is mostly null, including a heat-exercise study with substantial biochemical exposure but no performance benefit.[R2][R8]
- **Duration:** the cognition evidence is predominantly single-session; the 4-week safety study does not establish long-term cognitive benefit or long-term safety.[R2][R14]
- **Potentially adverse context:** higher exposure in older adults and a demanding cognitive-load paradigm produced poorer performance on selected tasks.[R9][R10]

## Mechanisms

The conservative mechanism is substrate availability, not direct dopamine-receptor stimulation.[R1][R2]

1. Acute stress, cold, prolonged wakefulness, or heavy cognitive demand may increase catecholamine requirements and alter catecholamine synthesis or release.[R1][R2]
2. Raising circulating free tyrosine can increase the tyrosine-to-competing-neutral-amino-acid ratio relevant to transport into the brain.[R11][R12]
3. Tyrosine hydroxylase can then use additional substrate to form L-DOPA, with downstream conversion to dopamine and norepinephrine.[R14]
4. Feedback inhibition and multiple rate-limiting steps constrain the size and duration of any central catecholamine increase.[R1][R2]
5. The resulting effect is more plausibly **prevention of a stress-related decrement** than enhancement above an already adequate baseline.[R1][R2]

The human studies generally measured performance and peripheral biomarkers rather than central brain catecholamines, so the catecholamine-preservation model remains mechanistic inference rather than a directly demonstrated explanation for every result.[R1][R2]

## Cautions

- The most recent controlled safety study enrolled healthy adult men and lasted 4 weeks; it found no serious adverse events and no clinically meaningful dose-related laboratory abnormalities at 1–4 g/day, but it does not establish safety for longer exposure, pregnancy, lactation, children, or people with significant disease.[R14]
- Higher doses are not automatically better: older adults had dose-related plasma increases accompanied by poorer working-memory performance in one trial.[R10]
- The thyroid relationship is biochemical: tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin participate in T4 and T3 synthesis.[R14] However, the American Thyroid Association guideline states that clinical or preclinical evidence for L-tyrosine having thyromimetic properties is absent.[R17]
- NALT has less direct human evidence than free L-tyrosine, and the available human precursor study was intravenous rather than oral.[R13]
- Supplement legal status and label accuracy do not establish efficacy or guarantee that a commercial product contains only the stated ingredient.[R18]

## Claims

| Claim | Evidence status |
|---|---|
| “L-tyrosine raises dopamine.” | It supplies substrate for dopamine synthesis, but the size and functional importance of any brain increase vary with state, dose, transport competition, and feedback regulation.[R1][R2][R14] |
| “It improves focus for everyone.” | Not supported; rested/control conditions can be null, and one cognitive-load trial found worse flexibility.[R3][R9] |
| “It counteracts sleep deprivation.” | Partly supported for selected short-lived task outcomes, but it does not restore sleep or normalize every cognitive measure.[R5][R6][R7] |
| “It improves exercise performance.” | Not consistently supported; heat-exercise performance was unchanged despite increased serum tyrosine.[R2][R8] |
| “NALT is more bioavailable than free L-tyrosine.” | Not established by the available human evidence; intravenous NALT showed substantial unchanged urinary excretion and only a modest free-tyrosine increase.[R13] |
| “It treats hypothyroidism.” | Not established; the ATA guideline reports no evidence that L-tyrosine has thyromimetic properties.[R17] |
| “It is proven safe long term.” | Not established; controlled safety data cited here extend to 4 weeks in healthy adult men.[R14] |

## Interactions

| Substance or context | Evidence status |
|---|---|
| **Phenelzine and related nonselective MAO-inhibitor labeling** | The current phenelzine label explicitly lists L-tyrosine among related compounds that patients should not take because MAO inhibition can potentiate sympathomimetic effects and cause hypertensive crises; the label does not quantify a tyrosine-specific incidence.[R15] |
| **Levodopa/carbidopa** | The carbidopa-levodopa label states that levodopa competes with certain amino acids for intestinal transport and that high-protein intake can impair absorption in some patients. The magnitude of any interaction from a discrete L-tyrosine supplement is not quantified in the cited label.[R16] |
| **Thyroid hormone therapy or thyroid disease** | Tyrosine participates in thyroid-hormone biosynthesis, but clinical thyromimetic activity has not been demonstrated. Direct supplement–thyroid-drug interaction data are insufficient in the cited clinical literature.[R14][R17] |
| **Protein meals and other large neutral amino acids** | These compounds share transport competition relevant to plasma ratios and brain entry; this is a nutrient-transport modifier rather than a conventional drug interaction.[R11][R12] |
| **NALT** | NALT should not be assumed to have the same exposure or interaction profile as free L-tyrosine because human conversion data are sparse and primarily intravenous.[R13] |

## Legal

In the United States, dietary supplements are not approved by FDA for safety, effectiveness, or labeling before marketing; manufacturers are responsible for product safety, truthful labeling, and compliance with applicable rules.[R18] Disease-treatment claims can cause a product to be regulated as a drug rather than as an ordinary dietary supplement.[R18] Legal status outside the United States, and the status of a specific NALT formulation, depends on jurisdiction, formulation, labeling, and intended use.
