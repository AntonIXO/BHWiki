# Coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinone and ubiquinol)

Slug: coenzyme-q10
Evidence cutoff: October 10, 2026
Source policy: primary human trials; systematic reviews/meta-analyses; official government/regulatory safety sources; pharmacovigilance; clinical-trial registries. No secondary consumer evidence was used for efficacy conclusions.

## Scope and evidence verdict

Coenzyme Q10 exists in supplements principally as ubiquinone, the oxidized form, and ubiquinol, the reduced form. Human evidence does not support treating “ubiquinol” as automatically clinically superior: a randomized crossover trial in older men found that 200 mg/day ubiquinol produced a larger rise in plasma CoQ10 than 200 mg/day ubiquinone, but neither formulation improved PBMC CoQ10, PBMC ATP, or the measured oxidative-stress biomarkers over two weeks. In other words, greater systemic exposure was demonstrated, not superior clinical efficacy. [1]

The strongest disease-treatment signal is in chronic heart failure, but it remains less secure than the headline effect sizes imply. Q-SYMBIO, the key 420-patient randomized trial, used 100 mg CoQ10 three times daily for two years and reported fewer major cardiovascular events, deaths and heart-failure hospitalizations, yet its 16-week NYHA, six-minute-walk and NT-proBNP endpoints were null. [2] Independent synthesis has emphasized high/unclear risk of bias in much of the literature, uncertain applicability and the need for a new independent adequately powered trial. [3]

For statin-associated muscle symptoms, the evidence is genuinely conflicting. The particularly informative trial that first verified symptoms by blinded simvastatin/placebo rechallenge found no benefit from 600 mg/day ubiquinol, despite a large serum-CoQ10 rise. [4] A smaller trial using water-soluble CoQ10 50 mg twice daily was positive. [5] The latest identified meta-analysis found a borderline pooled improvement in pain but extreme heterogeneity and instability to study selection. [6,7]

For migraine prevention, evidence is more consistently favorable but small-scale: 300 mg/day reduced several attack outcomes in a 42-person RCT, while a meta-analysis found fewer and shorter attacks but no clear reduction in headache severity. [8,9]

For healthy-person enhancement, the evidence is weak. A longer exercise trial was null for VO2max and lactate-threshold performance, whereas a tiny short-term fatigue study reported a task-specific benefit at 300 mg/day. [10,11] Most importantly, a 2026 randomized trial in 111 older adults found that 200 mg/day ubiquinol increased plasma CoQ10 roughly fourfold without improving cognition, mood, blood pressure, arterial stiffness, oxidative stress or inflammation. [12,13]

## Forms, formulation, pharmacokinetics, and dose

### Ubiquinone versus ubiquinol

Ubiquinone and ubiquinol are redox forms of the same CoQ10 molecule. In the registered Tufts crossover study NCT03020680, older men received each form at 200 mg/day for 14 days, separated by a two-week washout; the protocol specified administration with a main meal. [14] Ubiquinol increased plasma total CoQ10 more strongly, but responses varied by individual: six of ten completers responded more to ubiquinol while two responded more to ubiquinone. Neither formulation changed CoQ10 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells or the measured ATP/oxidative-stress markers. [1]

That study therefore supports higher plasma bioavailability for ubiquinol in that specific older-male population and formulation, not a general conclusion that ubiquinol produces better disease or enhancement outcomes.

### Formulation matters substantially

A seven-formulation, double-blind crossover experiment in 14 healthy young adults gave 100 mg per formulation and measured 48-hour plasma AUC. Bioavailability differed substantially; the two best-performing formulations included both an ubiquinone softgel and an ubiquinol softgel. Carrier lipids, solubilization and formulation components influenced absorption, and large inter-individual differences remained. [15]

A separate randomized trial in 60 healthy men found that oil-dissolved CoQ10 softgels raised serum concentrations more than the same nominal amount in crystalline powder form. 200 mg/day produced a larger increase than 100 mg/day, and 100 mg twice daily produced a larger serum increase than one 200-mg dose. Participants were instructed to take their capsules with meals. [16]

Accordingly, comparisons such as “ubiquinol versus ubiquinone” are incomplete unless the carrier, solubilization, softgel/tablet form and dosing schedule are also specified. [15]

### Human pharmacokinetics and food context

In an early isotope-tracer study, 16 healthy men received 100 mg deuterium-labelled CoQ10 orally. Mean peak plasma concentration occurred at approximately 6.5 ± 1.5 hours, terminal elimination half-life averaged 33.2 ± 5.3 hours, and many subjects had a second plasma peak around 24 hours, consistent with redistribution after hepatic uptake. [17]

A clean randomized fed-versus-fasted human pharmacokinetic comparison was not identified within the eligible evidence set. Some trials explicitly administered CoQ10 with meals, but that protocol choice cannot be converted into a quantitative food-effect estimate. [14,16]

## Clinical disease evidence

### Heart failure

Q-SYMBIO randomized 420 patients with moderate-to-severe chronic heart failure to CoQ10 100 mg three times daily or placebo in addition to conventional treatment. At 16 weeks, changes in NYHA class, six-minute walk distance and NT-proBNP were not significantly different. At two years, the composite major-adverse-cardiovascular-event outcome occurred in 15% versus 26% of patients, HR 0.50 (95% CI 0.32–0.80); cardiovascular mortality was 9% versus 16%, all-cause mortality 10% versus 18%, and heart-failure hospitalization was also lower. [2]

The distinction between outcomes matters: Q-SYMBIO is simultaneously negative for its early functional/biomarker endpoints and positive for its later clinical endpoints. [2]

The independent NIHR review found 26 trials involving 2,250 participants, many with high or unclear risk of bias. Its pooled all-cause-mortality estimate was RR 0.68 (95% CI 0.45–1.03), and short-term functional benefits were modest or unclear. The authors considered the treatment potentially clinically useful and cost-effective if the effect were real, but explicitly called for a new independent trial because of bias, plausibility and applicability concerns. [3] The NIHR work was publicly funded through the Health Technology Assessment programme. [21]

A later meta-analysis reported lower all-cause mortality and heart-failure hospitalization, but also documented major heterogeneity for LVEF and six-minute walk outcomes, variation in dose from 30–400 mg/day, and generally weak methodology among included trials. [22]

BHWiki assessment: promising adjunctive disease-treatment evidence, but not robust enough to turn Q-SYMBIO’s effect estimate into an expected individual benefit. Biomarkers, exercise capacity and clinical-event results should be reported separately.

### Statin-associated muscle symptoms

The Taylor et al. study is unusually informative because investigators first tested whether participants' previous statin symptoms were reproducible. Of 120 people reporting prior statin myalgia, only about 36% developed pain on simvastatin but not placebo during the blinded crossover confirmation. Forty-one then entered the treatment phase. [23]

Participants received ubiquinol 600 mg/day plus simvastatin 20 mg/day or placebo plus simvastatin. Serum CoQ10 rose from roughly 1.3 to 5.2 µg/mL with ubiquinol, yet pain severity/interference, muscle strength, VO2max and time to pain onset did not improve. This is direct evidence that a strong circulating-CoQ10 biomarker response did not translate into a symptom or performance response. [4]

By contrast, Skarlovnik et al. randomized 50 symptomatic statin users to water-soluble CoQ10 50 mg twice daily or placebo for 30 days and reported improvement in pain severity and pain interference without changes in liver/muscle enzymes or cholesterol. [24]

The 2025 meta-analysis included seven RCTs/389 participants using 100–600 mg/day for 30–90 days. Four trials were positive and three null; the pooled pain reduction was WMD −0.96 (95% CI −1.88 to −0.03). But heterogeneity was about I² 93%, and several leave-one-out analyses moved the confidence interval across zero. The review also noted that redox formulation was poorly reported and could not be evaluated as an effect modifier. [6,7]

BHWiki assessment: efficacy for SAMS is low-certainty and conflicting; “statins lower CoQ10” is not itself evidence that supplementation treats statin myalgia.

### Migraine

Sándor et al. compared CoQ10 100 mg three times daily with placebo in 42 people with migraine. During the third treatment month, attack frequency, headache days and nausea days favored CoQ10; the ≥50% attack-frequency responder rate was 47.6% versus 14.4%. [8]

A meta-analysis of six studies/371 participants subsequently found an estimated reduction of 1.52 attacks per month (95% CI −2.40 to −0.65) and shorter attack duration, but no statistically significant reduction in headache severity. [9]

BHWiki assessment: plausible preventive benefit for frequency/duration, with modest confidence because the underlying trials are small; do not extend the finding to acute migraine treatment.

### Diabetes and metabolic endpoints

A 74-person randomized factorial trial in type 2 diabetes/dyslipidemia tested 100 mg twice daily for 12 weeks. Plasma CoQ10 rose roughly threefold; systolic blood pressure fell by about 6 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure by about 3 mmHg and HbA1c by approximately 0.37 percentage points, while F2-isoprostanes did not change. [18]

The 2026 meta-analysis of 39 RCTs estimated fasting glucose −7.33 mg/dL and fasting insulin −0.84 µIU/mL. Its reported HbA1c estimate was approximately −0.12 percentage points with a 95% CI of −0.28 to +0.03, meaning the interval includes no effect despite positive wording in the abstract. The authors rated overall certainty very low. [25]

These are surrogate biomarkers. The evidence reviewed here does not establish prevention of myocardial infarction, stroke, nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy or mortality in diabetes.

## Healthy-person enhancement claims

### Exercise and fatigue

In 23 moderately trained healthy men, 90 mg/day for eight weeks produced no significant improvement in VO2max, workload at lactate threshold, heart rate at threshold, hypoxanthine, uric acid or creatine kinase. [10]

A much smaller 17-person crossover experiment using 100 or 300 mg/day for eight days reported less subjective fatigue and better preservation of a brief maximal cycling-velocity measure after prolonged exercise at 300 mg/day. [11]

These studies test different constructs—general aerobic fitness versus resistance to an experimentally induced fatigue protocol—and should not be pooled informally. The stronger practical conclusion is that general performance enhancement in healthy users has not been demonstrated robustly. [10,11]

### Cognition and healthy aging

The 2026 Nankivell et al. trial randomized 111 adults aged 60 years or older to ubiquinol 200 mg/day, given as 100 mg twice daily, for 90 days or placebo. Plasma CoQ10 at study end was approximately four times higher with ubiquinol. [12]

Despite this clear exposure change, there was no treatment benefit for the prespecified memory or processing-time composites, individual cognitive tests, subjective memory, mood, brachial or aortic blood pressure, arterial stiffness, oxidative stress or inflammatory biomarkers. [13]

Post-hoc within-group associations linked larger plasma-CoQ10 increases and lower oxidative stress to better memory in ubiquinol recipients, but those associations are not equivalent to a randomized treatment effect and should be treated as hypothesis-generating. [13]

BHWiki assessment: good evidence that 200 mg/day can raise plasma CoQ10 in healthy older adults; no convincing evidence that doing so improves short-term cognition.

## Safety, interactions, pregnancy, and organ impairment

NIH/NCCIH states that no serious CoQ10 adverse effects have been reported in its safety summary and lists digestive upset and insomnia as possible mild effects. It also cautions that CoQ10 may interact with warfarin and insulin and may be incompatible with some cancer treatments. [26]

The 2026 healthy-older-adult trial recorded similar overall adverse-event counts between ubiquinol and placebo and identified no treatment-related serious adverse event. [13] Heart-failure meta-analysis likewise did not demonstrate an increase in adverse events, although the confidence interval remained imprecise. [22]

### Warfarin

Twenty-four stable long-term warfarin patients entered a randomized double-blind crossover trial of CoQ10 100 mg/day, Ginkgo biloba and placebo, each for four weeks with washouts. INR remained stable and mean weekly warfarin requirement did not differ materially during CoQ10 versus placebo. [19]

That small negative interaction trial does not justify declaring the combination interaction-free, particularly because NIH/NCCIH still carries the precaution. [26] For BHWiki, the balanced label is “possible interaction; controlled evidence limited and negative; monitor when started/stopped in warfarin users.”

### Renal and hepatic considerations

The phenotype-confirmed SAMS trial excluded renal disease defined by creatinine >2 mg/dL and hepatic disease with ALT >2× the upper limit of normal. [4] The ubiquinone-versus-ubiquinol PK registry likewise excluded people with impaired renal function or diseases/medications likely to alter gastrointestinal absorption. [14]

Therefore there is no evidence-based renal or hepatic dose-adjustment algorithm in this evidence base. This is a data gap, not evidence that dose adjustment is unnecessary.

### Pregnancy

Teran et al. randomized 235 women at increased risk of pre-eclampsia to CoQ10 200 mg/day from 20 weeks of pregnancy until delivery or placebo. Pre-eclampsia occurred in 14.4% versus 25.6%, RR 0.56 (95% CI 0.33–0.96). [20]

The corresponding registry, NCT00300937, lists 235 actual enrollees and collaborators including the Ecuadorian Foundation for Science and Technology, Universidad Central del Ecuador and Jarrow Formulas Inc. [27]

This establishes exposure during later pregnancy in one defined high-risk population; it does not establish general pregnancy, fetal, neonatal or breastfeeding safety.

### Pharmacovigilance

A Swedish Medical Products Agency analysis reviewed spontaneous reports associated with complementary/alternative medicines from 1987–2006. Such pharmacovigilance can identify possible safety signals but has no reliable exposure denominator and cannot generate an incidence rate or prove causality. The publication aggregates many substances and should not be presented as a CoQ10-specific risk estimate. [28]
