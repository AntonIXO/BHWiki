## Identity, formulations, and dose conversion

Pure anhydrous choline chloride consists of the approximately 104.17-g/mol choline cation plus the 35.45-g/mol chloride counterion. From the 139.63-g/mol salt molecular mass, the theoretical choline-cation mass fraction is **~74.6%**. Thus 1,000 mg pure anhydrous choline chloride supplies about **746 mg choline-equivalent**; conversely, 550 mg choline-equivalent corresponds to about 737 mg pure salt. This agrees with the human PK study’s nominal **740 mg choline chloride supplying 550 mg choline**. “Elemental choline” is best avoided: the unambiguous terms are **choline-equivalent mass** and **choline-chloride salt mass**. [R1,R7]

This distinction matters substantially in the prenatal literature. The Cornell feeding trial’s **100 and 550 mg/day arms were supplemental choline amounts delivered from choline chloride**, not 100 and 550 mg of salt. With a basal diet of about 380 mg/day choline, those interventions produced total intakes of approximately 480 and 930 mg/day; stoichiometrically the supplements correspond to about **134 and 737 mg/day anhydrous choline chloride**, respectively. [R12]

EFSA’s 2025 animal-feed assessment specifies choline chloride active substance at **≥99% purity on an anhydrous basis** and describes commercial manufacture as concentrated aqueous solution plus solid preparations made with carriers. [R1] Current EU feed legislation authorizes an **aqueous solution containing at least 74% choline chloride** and solid feed preparations, while identifying the active substance as chemically synthesized solid choline chloride of ≥99% anhydrous purity. The authorization is explicitly for **animal nutrition** and includes feed/water-use conditions; it is not a human supplement specification. [R2]

Accordingly, a 75% choline-chloride aqueous feed solution contains about **560 mg choline-equivalent per gram of solution**, whereas a hypothetical 50%-choline-chloride carrier preparation contains about **373 mg choline-equivalent per gram**. These are formulation conversions only, not human dose recommendations. Feed-grade carrier preparations should not be assumed suitable for direct human consumption simply because the active molecule is identical. [R1,R2]

The current EU authorization also reflects product-quality concerns that are specific to industrial synthesis and feed use: EFSA assessed manufacturing impurities and regarded choline chloride active substance as a potential skin and respiratory sensitizer; the Commission’s 2026 authorization was subsequently changed/corrected. These regulatory specifications should not be silently substituted for human-food or pharmacopeial specifications. [R1,R2]

## Human pharmacokinetics and biomarker kinetics

### Oral choline chloride

The strongest direct oral PK comparison is a prospective randomized crossover study in **six healthy adult men**. Each participant received 550 mg choline-equivalent once as choline chloride, choline bitartrate, Alpha-GPC, and egg phosphatidylcholine in randomized sequence. The choline-chloride treatment was **740 mg salt**, dissolved in water and taken with a small test meal; blood was sampled through 24 hours. [R7] [R7]

For choline chloride specifically, median plasma-free-choline **Tmax was 2.25 h** (IQR 0.88–3.5 h) and betaine Tmax 2.5 h. Median choline AUC was **63.4 µmol/L·h over 0–6 h** and **235.2 µmol/L·h over 0–24 h**. Across all supplementation periods, median plasma free choline rose from about 8.75 to 14.51 µmol/L and betaine from 20.4 to 33.1 µmol/L, then returned close to baseline by 24 hours. [R7]

Importantly, a single 550-mg choline-equivalent dose did **not** produce a corresponding acute rise in total plasma phosphatidylcholine, lyso-PC, sphingomyelin, GPC, phosphocholine, dimethylglycine, methionine, or carnitine in that small study. Plasma free choline is therefore not interchangeable with total phosphatidylcholine as a kinetic endpoint. [R7]

A subsequent randomized crossover isotope study, again in **six healthy men**, administered 2.7 mg/kg D9-choline-equivalent as D9-choline chloride, D9-phosphocholine, D9-GPC, or D9-POPC. With D9-choline chloride, labelled plasma free choline peaked at about **1 h**, labelled betaine around **1.5 h**, and D9-free-choline had essentially returned to zero by approximately 5 h. In marked contrast, D9-labelled phosphatidylcholine derived from the water-soluble choline sources showed a delayed plateau/peak around **24–33 h**; the tabulated median for D9-choline chloride was about **28.5 h**. [R8] [R8]

### TMAO

In the 550-mg-equivalent crossover trial, the water-soluble preparations—including choline chloride—produced an acute rise in plasma TMAO, with large between-person variation; egg phosphatidylcholine did not show the same response. Across supplementation experiments TMAO rose from a median around 2.0 µmol/L to 6.73 µmol/L at 6 h and was again around baseline by 24 h. The isotope study likewise detected labelled TMAO after D9-choline chloride, while showing substantially different TMAO production among choline forms. [R7,R8]

These are **plasma biomarker findings**, not cardiovascular events. Neither direct choline-chloride PK trial established that its transient TMAO increase causes myocardial infarction, stroke, thrombosis, or other patient-important harm. No qualifying randomized human choline-chloride trial identified here directly tested platelet reactivity or cardiovascular clinical outcomes. A TMAO increase should therefore be recorded as a metabolic effect, **not converted into a clinical-harm rating**. [R7,R8]

### Intravenous PK and the half-life problem

In four adults receiving long-term total parenteral nutrition, Buchman and colleagues infused **7, 14, 28, and 56 mmol choline chloride over 12 hours on successive days**. Those doses correspond approximately to **0.98, 1.95, 3.91, and 7.82 g choline-chloride salt**, respectively. Plasma and urinary choline were serially measured. [R9]

The concentration data fit a **two-compartment model with saturable elimination from the central compartment** significantly better than a one-compartment model in all four subjects; the saturable model also fit better than a nonsaturating model in three of four. [R9]

Consequently, the existing BHWiki record is correct to reject a single universal choline-chloride “half-life.” The approximately 1-h free-choline turnover language in later studies, the disappearance of labelled free choline by ~5 h, and return of unlabeled concentrations toward baseline by 6–24 h are **not validated terminal elimination half-lives**. With multicompartment distribution and saturable elimination, a concentration-independent single \(t_{1/2}\) is not established by the human choline-chloride literature retrieved here. [R9] [R8]

## Cognitive, prenatal, disease, and parenteral evidence

### Healthy cognition, attention, visuomotor, and exercise claims

Direct choline-chloride studies do **not** establish a general healthy-user nootropic effect. Nine young adults receiving **2 g choline chloride four times daily (8 g/day)** in a placebo–drug–placebo design showed no significant average improvement in memory storage or retrieval, although a post hoc correlation suggested that some individuals who had previously responded to physostigmine might respond slightly better. [R17]

Ten nondemented adults over age 60 also received **2 g four times daily** for 21 days. Choline chloride did not significantly improve memory storage, retrieval, or digit span. [R18] An earlier eight-person study using **16 g/day for 7 days** likewise found no average memory advantage over placebo periods, despite one poor-baseline performer improving. [R17]

Disease evidence is similarly cautionary. In seven patients with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer disease, choline chloride at **50, 100, and 200 mg/kg/day** in a double-blind crossover design failed to produce significant overall cognitive improvement despite more than doubling plasma choline. [R19]

Two positive studies frequently discoverable under generic “choline cognition” searches must **not** be imported into this record. The placebo-controlled 2015 visuomotor study used **2 g choline bitartrate**, not chloride. [R20] The 2015 acute healthy-volunteer neurocognitive study used **500 or 1,000 mg CDP-choline/citicoline**, not choline chloride. [R21]

Likewise, the evidence set reviewed here did not yield a qualifying chloride-specific exercise-performance trial sufficient to support ergogenic claims. Results obtained with other choline salts or phospholipid forms should not be reassigned to choline chloride because oral metabolism and TMAO kinetics demonstrably differ among formulations. [R7] [R8]

### Prenatal controlled feeding and offspring outcomes

The most informative prenatal chloride trial is **NCT01127022**, a Cornell randomized controlled-feeding program. During late pregnancy, the basal diet supplied approximately 380 mg/day choline; supplemental choline from choline chloride was either **100 mg/day** or **550 mg/day**, producing total intakes of approximately **480 or 930 mg/day**. The registry lists Cornell as lead sponsor and the American Egg Board, National Cattlemen’s Beef Association/Beef Checkoff, and USDA Beltsville Human Nutrition Research Center as collaborators. [R12]

In the maternal/fetal biomarker publication, healthy pregnant women beginning around 27 weeks and nonpregnant controls were randomized to the two intake levels for 12 weeks. Higher intake produced higher concentrations of several choline-related metabolites and approximately doubled cord-plasma dimethylglycine. These findings demonstrate altered maternal/fetal metabolism; they are **biomarkers, not proof of improved pregnancy or neonatal clinical outcomes**. [R13]

Stable-isotope work from the same feeding program showed that pregnancy altered choline partitioning toward phosphatidylcholine synthesis and increased use of choline-derived methyl groups, with selective fetal transfer patterns. Again, this is mechanistic human metabolic evidence rather than a patient-important endpoint. [R13]

Offspring cognition provides the most notable patient-relevant signal. Among **24 infants** evaluated longitudinally at 4, 7, 10, and 13 months, infants born to women assigned 930 mg/day total choline had faster average information-processing reaction time than those in the 480-mg/day group. [R14]

At age seven, only **20 children** from the original trial were evaluated. The higher maternal-intake group performed better on the prespecified sustained-attention score and showed less vigilance decrement; for the briefest visual signals, hit rates declined 22.9% over the session in the 480-mg/day group versus a 1.5% increase in the 930-mg/day group. False alarms, omissions, and off-task behavior did not differ. The small follow-up sample materially limits certainty. [R15]

A separate free-living prenatal trial randomized participants from gestational week 12–16 through delivery to **550 mg/day supplemental choline as choline chloride**—500 mg unlabeled plus 50 mg methyl-D9 choline—or a 25-mg/day labelled-choline control. The higher dose increased plasma free choline, betaine, phosphatidylcholine-related measures and several placental/cord biomarkers at one or more timepoints. Its primary contribution is metabolic, not demonstration of a child clinical benefit. [R16]

These prenatal findings cannot legitimately be reframed as evidence that choline chloride acutely improves attention in healthy adults: the intervention occurred during fetal neurodevelopment, the maternal total-choline contrast was 480 versus 930 mg/day, and the informative child studies were small longitudinal follow-ups. [R14] [R15]

### Parenteral-nutrition deficiency and repletion

Four adults who had received TPN for a mean of approximately 9.7 years and had low plasma free choline received **1–4 g/day intravenous choline chloride for six weeks**. Plasma free choline entered the reference range within one week. CT evidence of hepatic steatosis improved substantially during treatment; one patient later demonstrated recurrence after return to choline-free PN. [R10]

This is clinically relevant deficiency-repletion evidence but remains an **uncontrolled four-person series**, with CT liver attenuation rather than hard hepatic outcomes. It should not be extrapolated to supplementation of choline-replete users. [R10]

A subsequent randomized pilot enrolled 11 long-term TPN patients: six continued usual PN and five received usual PN plus **2 g choline chloride during the nightly infusion for 24 weeks**. Delayed visual recall improved significantly in the supplemented arm, whereas the CVLT List B and Trails A results were only borderline and the numerous other neuropsychological measures did not significantly change. The title’s suggestion of broad “verbal and visual memory” improvement therefore overstates the statistically secure findings somewhat. [R11]

The coherent clinical interpretation is that choline-chloride repletion can be useful in documented long-term PN-associated choline deficiency; that evidence is categorically different from healthy-user cognitive enhancement. [R10] [R11]

## Safety, special populations, and interactions

Official NIH nutrient guidance lists **fishy body odor, vomiting, excessive sweating/salivation, hypotension, and liver toxicity** among adverse effects associated with excessive total choline intake. These are choline-level safety findings rather than effects uniquely proven for choline chloride. [R6]

The U.S. tolerable upper intake level for **total choline** is 3,500 mg/day for adults; age-based ULs are 3,000 mg/day at 14–18 years, 2,000 mg/day at 9–13 years, and 1,000 mg/day at 1–8 years; ULs are not established for infants. Pregnancy and lactation use the corresponding age-based UL rather than a special choline-chloride ceiling. [R6]

Stoichiometrically, 3,500 mg choline corresponds to roughly **4.69 g pure anhydrous choline chloride**, but this must **not** be presented as a recommended “safe dose of choline chloride”: the dietary UL applies to total choline exposure from all sources. Old direct cognition trials using 8–16 g/day salt therefore delivered choline far above the modern adult UL. [R18] [R17]

GI intolerance is directly documented with the chloride salt: diarrhea was an indexed adverse effect in the Alzheimer crossover trial, whose highest dose reached 200 mg/kg/day. [R19] Conversely, **liver toxicity should not be presented as a routine choline-chloride effect**: official guidance concerns excessive total choline, while in severely choline-deficient TPN patients IV choline chloride actually improved steatosis. [R10]

For pregnancy and lactation, nutrient requirements are specified as total choline. EFSA sets adequate intakes of **400 mg/day for adults, 480 mg/day during pregnancy, and 520 mg/day during lactation**. [R5] U.S. guidance uses 450 mg/day during pregnancy and 550 mg/day during lactation. [R6] These are dietary-reference values, not choline-chloride drug doses.

No dedicated human chloride-specific PK or dose-adjustment study was identified for **chronic renal impairment** or **chronic hepatic impairment**. The modern oral trials enrolled healthy men, the prenatal trial excluded relevant kidney/liver problems, and the PN studies addressed deficiency rather than organ-impairment PK. Consequently, there is no evidence-based renal or hepatic choline-chloride adjustment algorithm in this evidence set. [R7] [R12]

For medications, NIH nutrient guidance states that choline is **not known to have clinically relevant medication interactions**. [R6] More specifically, no qualifying direct human trial identified here establishes a clinically important choline-chloride interaction with **anticholinergics, cholinesterase inhibitors, or levodopa**. The old physostigmine/choline work evaluated response correlations rather than establishing a concurrent drug interaction, so cholinergic mechanism alone should not be converted into an interaction warning. [R17]

Other choline sources do matter for **total choline exposure** and the UL, but their salt masses are not additive on a milligram-for-milligram basis. A product supplying 500 mg choline “from choline bitartrate,” for example, cannot be counted as 500 mg choline chloride, and formulation-specific PK/TMAO findings show why the compounds should remain separate. [R7]

### Wilson disease and iron: identity correction

Ordinary choline chloride is **not** the Wilson-disease investigational drug **bis-choline tetrathiomolybdate**, also known as ALXN1840/WTX101. The phase 3 Wilson-disease registry explicitly lists ALXN1840’s alternate names as “tiomolibdic acid,” “tiomolibdate choline,” and “bis-choline tetrathiomolybdate,” with doses of the distinct molybdenum-containing active substance. The program was sponsored by Alexion Pharmaceuticals and was subsequently terminated by sponsor decision. [R22]

Accordingly, copper-chelation indications and metal/iron interaction precautions belonging to bis-choline tetrathiomolybdate—or to other Wilson therapies such as trientine, penicillamine, or zinc—must **not** be imported into the choline-chloride record. The targeted human search found no qualifying choline-chloride/iron interaction study. [R22]

## Regulatory status, product quality, and evidence integrity

In the United States, **21 CFR §182.8252** specifically names choline chloride and states that it is generally recognized as safe when used according to good manufacturing practice. The eCFR page was current through October 2026. This is a **food-ingredient GRAS provision**, not FDA approval of choline chloride as a therapeutic drug for cognition, exercise performance, liver disease, or Wilson disease. [R3]

USP also sells a **Choline Chloride Reference Standard, USP-1133547**, specified for use in official USP-NF dietary-supplement tests and assays. Existence of a reference standard supports identity/assay control; it does not establish that an arbitrary bulk or feed product is USP-grade. [R4]

EU legislation is especially useful for preventing the human-food/feed conflation. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/164 explicitly renewed an aqueous choline-chloride solution and choline-chloride preparation as **feed additives for all animal species**, classed as nutritional feed additives. It specifies ≥74% choline chloride for the aqueous solution and ≥99% anhydrous-basis purity for the active substance, and imposes feed/water and worker-protection conditions. [R2] Those are not human clinical-product specifications.

The evidence also has identifiable sponsorship patterns. The 2022 oral PK comparison had only six men and was partly supported by **HiPP-Werk Georg Hipp OHG**; the authors reported no conflicts. [R7] The Cornell prenatal trial was investigator-led but its registry lists the **American Egg Board, National Cattlemen’s Beef Association/Beef Checkoff, and USDA Beltsville Human Nutrition Research Center** as collaborators. [R12] The 1990s–2001 PN studies are indexed as receiving U.S. PHS and/or other governmental research support, but modern-style conflict disclosures were not available in the retrieved historical records. [R9] [R10] [R11]

The EFSA feed opinion is a regulator-authored assessment, but the underlying renewal dossier was generated by a consortium of three commercial choline-chloride producers/distributors. EFSA explicitly states this provenance. [R1] This is appropriately classified as **industry-submitted regulatory evidence reviewed by an independent public regulator**, rather than as wholly independent primary experimentation.

Overall, three seemingly conflicting evidence streams become coherent when identity and population are respected: oral choline chloride reliably raises **biomarkers** such as free choline/betaine and can raise TMAO; choline-deficient long-term PN patients can improve with **repletion**; but direct healthy/elderly choline-chloride cognition studies are largely **null**, while favorable prenatal offspring findings concern developmental exposure during pregnancy and small follow-up cohorts. [R7] [R10] [R18] [R14]

The BHWiki record should therefore retain four hard boundaries: **do not infer cardiovascular harm from TMAO alone; do not infer cognitive efficacy from acetylcholine mechanisms; do not transfer clinical findings among chemically different choline forms; and do not treat feed-grade formulation specifications as human-product quality standards.**
