## Evidence summary

Magnesium L-threonate (MgT; Magtein® is the best-studied branded material) is a magnesium salt containing a relatively small amount of elemental magnesium by weight. EFSA characterizes the novel-food material as magnesium L-threonate monohydrate, with 7.2–8.3% magnesium; its assessed maximum intake of 3,000 mg Mg L-threonate/day corresponds to about 250 mg elemental magnesium and 2,730 mg L-threonate. NIH emphasizes that supplement labels report elemental magnesium, not the total mass of the magnesium salt. [1][2]

The human evidence is promising but not robust. Positive cognitive signals appeared in a small 2016 randomized trial of older adults, a newer six-week randomized trial in younger adults with dissatisfied sleep, and a 2022 trial of a multi-ingredient Magtein formulation. Individual cognitive endpoints were often null, an independent 2023 randomized breast-cancer-survivor trial found no cognitive benefit, and much of the positive evidence has substantial commercial sponsorship or author-industry involvement. [3][4][5][6]

Sleep evidence is mixed. The 2024 *Sleep Medicine: X* trial found improvements in selected Oura-derived scores and several subjective/daytime outcomes, but its overall ISI treatment-by-time interaction was not significant, many sleep subscales were null, and placebo responses were substantial. A separate six-week trial reported improvement in subjective sleep-related impairment but not objective Oura sleep measures. The 2016 trial could not distinguish a sleep effect from placebo, while the 2023 breast-cancer trial found no PSQI benefit. [3][6][7]

## Brain-magnesium claims, mechanisms, and pharmacokinetics

The common claim that MgT has uniquely high “brain bioavailability” originates mainly from preclinical work. EFSA’s official assessment cites a rat study reporting roughly 60% relative magnesium absorption from Mg L-threonate versus about 40–45% for chloride, gluconate and citrate under those conditions, and another rat experiment reporting increased CSF magnesium after MgT administration—about 7% versus baseline and 16% versus control at day 24. These are animal findings, not human BBB measurements. [1]

The 2016 randomized trial measured plasma, red-cell and urinary magnesium. EFSA reports urinary magnesium rising 41% versus 8% with placebo at week 12 (p=0.048); plasma magnesium showed a transient week-6 difference but no meaningful week-12 between-group difference, and RBC magnesium was not consistently significantly different from placebo. These measurements establish oral magnesium bioavailability but do not establish increased human brain or CSF magnesium. [1]

Human pharmacokinetic information on MgT itself is sparse. EFSA cites a human study of the L-threonate moiety administered as calcium L-threonate, not magnesium L-threonate: mean plasma threonate half-life about 2.5 h, fasting Tmax around 2 h, fed Tmax around 3 h; food increased AUC about 33% and Cmax about 21% at the studied dose, and four days of repeated dosing produced no accumulation. This is threonate disposition, not MgT magnesium pharmacokinetics and cannot prove CNS magnesium delivery. [1]

**Clean distinction:** measured animal finding—MgT can increase CSF magnesium in rodents and produce synaptic/plasticity effects in preclinical models; measured human finding—oral MgT changes peripheral magnesium handling and has produced some clinical cognitive/sleep signals; not measured sufficiently in humans—superior BBB penetration, increased human CSF/brain magnesium, or greater CNS delivery than other magnesium salts.

NMDA-receptor regulation, synaptic density/plasticity, neuronal energy metabolism, GABA and melatonin should remain mechanism/hypothesis fields rather than clinical-outcome evidence. The 2016 human paper itself describes its synaptic-density rationale as arising from preclinical work. [4]

## Safety, renal function, and medication binding

EFSA concluded that assessed magnesium L-threonate monohydrate is a bioavailable magnesium source and safe under proposed conditions of use at up to 3 g/day for adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women, corresponding to approximately 250 mg elemental magnesium/day. Subsequent EU authorization permitted magnesium L-threonate as a novel food. [1][9]

The controlled 2016 study reported 47 adverse events in 28 of 51 safety participants; EFSA described all as mild. Gastrointestinal events affected 5/25 active versus 4/26 placebo participants. Active-treatment events judged possibly or probably related included loose stools, dry mouth, stomach upset and hypertension. The study was small and excluded important higher-risk groups. [1]

NIH states that kidneys are central to magnesium homeostasis and toxicity risk rises with impaired renal function or kidney failure because magnesium clearance is reduced. MgT trials commonly excluded renal impairment; a registered schizophrenia MgT trial explicitly excluded kidney dysfunction on this basis. [2][10]

NIH’s U.S. adult UL is 350 mg/day elemental magnesium from supplements and medications, excluding naturally occurring food magnesium. EFSA uses a lower 250 mg/day supplemental-magnesium UL in its European assessment. These regulatory thresholds are not efficacy doses. [1][2]

Medication interactions are primarily magnesium-ion class effects, not a demonstrated threonate-specific phenomenon:

- magnesium-containing supplements can reduce oral bisphosphonate absorption; NIH advises separating by at least 2 h;
- magnesium forms insoluble complexes with tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics; NIH advises taking those antibiotics at least 2 h before or 4–6 h after magnesium;
- loop and thiazide diuretics can increase urinary magnesium loss, whereas potassium-sparing diuretics reduce magnesium excretion;
- long-term proton-pump inhibitor therapy can produce hypomagnesemia; in some FDA-reviewed cases supplementation alone did not correct it;
- high supplemental magnesium exposure can cause diarrhea, nausea and abdominal cramping; severe hypermagnesemia can progress to hypotension, weakness, respiratory difficulty, arrhythmia and cardiac arrest, with renal impairment increasing risk. [2]

## Distinction from other magnesium salts and current trials

MgT should not be equated with magnesium glycinate, citrate, chloride, oxide, sulfate or other formulations simply on the basis of elemental dose. It supplies a different ligand and a relatively low percentage of elemental magnesium. Some early L-TAMS clinical studies used an anhydrous material rather than the monohydrate characterized by EFSA. [1]

NIH states that forms dissolving well in liquid tend to be absorbed better and that human studies have generally found aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride more bioavailable than oxide and sulfate. NIH does not state that MgT is more bioavailable than all other salts or uniquely brain-penetrant in humans. [2]

No published human trial identified here directly establishes that MgT is superior to magnesium glycinate, citrate, chloride or oxide for sleep or cognition. UCLA registry NCT07640685 is designed to compare magnesium glycinate vs magnesium L-threonate vs placebo in varsity athletes, but is listed without results. [11]

NCT07706283 (Barry University) enrolled 81 healthy adults for six weeks to study sleep, cognition and neuromuscular function; it was listed completed in March 2026 without posted results. NCT07015047 (UCLA) studies sleep/recovery and athletic performance in 100 collegiate athletes and was listed active, not recruiting, without posted results. Absence of posted results is neither positive nor negative evidence. [12][13]

**Key unresolved evidence gaps:** direct human CSF/brain-magnesium measurements; a completed published MgT-versus-other-magnesium-salt trial; long-term cognitive outcomes; clinically meaningful dementia/Alzheimer disease endpoints; and adequate safety data in chronic kidney disease, pregnancy/lactation, and people taking interacting medicines. The strongest “brain magnesium” evidence remains preclinical rather than directly demonstrated in humans. [1]
