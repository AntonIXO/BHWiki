# Phenylpiracetam (Fonturacetam; Phenotropil/Actitropil) — BHWiki Deep Research

Slug: phenylpiracetam
Evidence cutoff: October 10, 2026
Scope: racemic fonturacetam versus R-/S-enantiomers and MRZ-9547; patient indications, healthy-user claims, formulation/dose, safety, interactions, regulatory status, sponsorship and conflicts.

## Identity and evidence boundary

The clinically used Russian drug fonturacetam/phenylpiracetam is the racemate. The stereoselective pharmacology literature explicitly describes Phenotropil as clinically used in racemic form, while current Russian Fonturacetam-Akrikhin labeling names the active ingredient simply as fonturacetam and supplies 100-mg tablets without an R- or S-enantiomer designation. Clinical results for Phenotropil, Actitropil and ordinary fonturacetam tablets therefore belong to the racemate and should not be assigned to isolated R- or S-phenylpiracetam. [R11][R15]

R-phenylpiracetam/MRZ-9547 is a separate evidence object. Preclinical publications describe MRZ-9547 as the R-configured phenylpiracetam enantiomer and as a dopamine-transporter-active compound. The PubMed literature located for MRZ-9547 consists of animal/in-vitro studies rather than human efficacy trials; a ClinicalTrials.gov search for MRZ-9547/R-phenylpiracetam did not return a registered study in the search performed for this record. Accordingly, historical secondary claims about MRZ-9547 having undergone human Phase I development should not be imported into BHWiki as established clinical evidence without a primary registry or regulator record. [R12][R13]

S-phenylpiracetam is also distinct. Its published evidence in the allowed set is preclinical; a 2017 rodent study describes S-phenylpiracetam as DAT-active and reports metabolic/weight effects, but it supplies no human cognition, fatigue, athletic-performance or safety evidence. [R14]

Regulated Russian tablets should likewise be distinguished from unverified online powders/capsules or products marketed as “R-phenylpiracetam.” The current Akrikhin source specifies a prescription 100-mg fonturacetam tablet, composition and official leaflet. Nothing in that label establishes the identity, dose accuracy, purity or enantiomeric composition of unrelated online products; under the user-specified source policy, no regulator-verified analytical series of current online phenylpiracetam products was included. [R15]

## Human clinical evidence

### Cerebral ischemia and stroke rehabilitation

The largest readily identifiable stroke study enrolled 400 patients with ischemic stroke. Two hundred received phenotropil 400 mg/day, in three courses during the first year after stroke, as part of complex rehabilitation. Recovery was measured with Barthel, Lindmark, Scandinavian, and Merton/Sutton scales, and the authors reported significantly better neurologic and activities-of-daily-living recovery in the phenotropil group (p<0.0001). The PubMed record classifies it as a controlled clinical trial but does not state that allocation was randomized or blinded, and treatment was added to a rehabilitation program rather than tested as isolated monotherapy. [R2]

A 2005 comparative study involved 99 patients with residual encephalopathy after cerebrovascular events, traumatic brain injury or glioma surgery. Phenotropil was given at 200 mg/day for one month. The abstract reports improvements in motor deficits, coordination, higher cognitive functions, memory, attention, daily activity, anxiety/depression and EEG measures, but the mixed etiologies and limited comparator detail make it weak evidence for stroke rehabilitation specifically. [R3]

Additional older Russian publications address post-stroke sequelae and vascular encephalopathy, but the PubMed records contain little or no abstract-level information about allocation, blinding, dose, effect size or adverse events; they are best regarded as supportive historical signals rather than confirmatory trials. [R4][R5]

BHWiki assessment: evidence for stroke/cerebrovascular rehabilitation is positive but low-certainty: geographically concentrated, mostly older, incompletely reported, and lacking the trial-design transparency needed to distinguish a pharmacologic effect from rehabilitation intensity, selection and other co-interventions. [R2][R3]

### Epilepsy

The strongest described epilepsy study is a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial of 90 adults with symptomatic focal epilepsy receiving standard antiseizure therapy plus phenotropil 100 or 200 mg or placebo. The authors report reduced seizure frequency and better cognitive function. The abstract does not give a numerical seizure-rate treatment effect and also reports that phenotropil did not reduce adverse/negative effects of background antiseizure treatment in 40% of patients. [R6]

A separate multicenter comparative study enrolled 31 patients receiving phenotropil 100 mg/day for two months plus antiseizure medication and 30 controls receiving antiseizure therapy alone. It reported a significant decrease in seizures and improvement in EEG epileptiform indices, but randomization and blinding are not stated in the PubMed abstract. [R7]

A 2013 randomized study of 75 patients with symptomatic focal post-traumatic epilepsy reported improvements in seizure burden, cognition, quality of life, depression and EEG variables and noted an absence of the seizure aggravation sometimes associated with nootropics. However, the abstract discusses both phenotropil and mexidol and does not cleanly supply a phenotropil-only effect size or dosing comparison. [R8]

These are add-on, not replacement-treatment, studies. The evidence does not justify substituting fonturacetam for established antiseizure therapy, and the small trial base lacks large independent replication. [R6][R7]

### Asthenia and fatigue

The most comprehensive synthesis is a 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 11 publications comprising 549 patients. All included treatment estimates analyzed fonturacetam at 200 mg/day. After one month, MFI-20 scores fell by a mean 16.3 points (95% CI 8.85–23.85; p<0.0001); the review additionally reports favorable changes in mood, sleep, cognition and quality of life. Mean adverse-event frequency was 5.5%, with reported events described as transient. [R1]

The important limitation is that the headline meta-analytic quantity is essentially change during treatment, not a pooled placebo-controlled treatment difference. Given that much of the underlying Russian literature is observational/open-label, a large before-versus-after improvement cannot by itself separate drug effect from regression to the mean, spontaneous recovery, expectancy, co-intervention or selective publication. That limitation materially lowers certainty despite the statistically large estimate. [R1]

The TRIUMPH observational program included 1,170 patients with chronic brain ischemia and asthenia, aged 45–65. The PubMed abstract reports a 100-mg phenotropil treatment dose over two to three months, MFI-20 improvement by month one, and more than twofold symptom-score reduction by month three. It was explicitly a non-interventional observational program, so it provides a large treatment signal but not a causal placebo-controlled estimate. [R9]

A useful conflicting result comes from cardiovascular patients with neurotic, somatoform, stress-associated or depressive disorders: phenotropil was generally described as tolerable, but its treatment effect—particularly the anxiolytic component—declined after 4–8 weeks, and the comparator had advantages on several measures. [R10]

## Healthy-person cognition and athletic enhancement

As of the evidence cutoff, the literature search located no published primary human phenylpiracetam trial demonstrating cognitive enhancement in healthy volunteers. The relevant 2026 registry is therefore important precisely because it should not yet be treated as positive evidence. ClinicalTrials.gov study NCT07852130 enrolled 70 healthy adults aged 18–35 in a placebo-controlled Phase 1 study involving modafinil, oxiracetam, pramiracetam, phenylpiracetam and aniracetam. The phenylpiracetam arm used oral 100, 200 or 300 mg/day before testing, with approximately nine participants per active compound. [R16]

The registry gives a start date of March 9, 2026, primary completion September 9, completion September 12, 2026, and first posting on October 1, 2026—after study completion. Its active arms were very small, and the registry itself describes an estimated minimum detectable standardized effect of about d=1.2 or greater. This retrospective posting materially limits verification that the eventual analysis reflects prospectively registered outcomes. [R16]

Most importantly, as of October 10, 2026 the registry reports `has_posted_results=false`, no results modules and no posted outcomes; the linked publications are background references rather than publications of this trial. Thus the study currently supplies zero usable efficacy or safety result for healthy-user phenylpiracetam. [R16][R17]

Russian labeling nevertheless includes prevention of fatigue and improvement of mental/physical work capacity in extreme professional settings. For “performance enhancement,” the current Fonturacetam-Akrikhin leaflet gives 100–200 mg once in the morning for two weeks; for athletes, three days. This is a label instruction, not a controlled healthy-athlete efficacy result. [R15]

For competitive sport, fonturacetam/4-phenylpiracetam/carphedon is included in WADA’s 2026 Prohibited List under S6 stimulants and is prohibited in competition. Anti-doping classification should not be misrepresented as proof of performance enhancement. [R17]

## Dose, formulation and pharmacokinetic provenance

The current official Akrikhin product information describes Fonturacetam-Akrikhin 100-mg oral tablets, prescription-only, with 30-, 60- and 90-tablet presentations. Each tablet contains 100 mg fonturacetam; the listed excipients include microcrystalline cellulose, povidone K30, sodium carboxymethyl starch and magnesium stearate. [R15]

The current leaflet gives an average single dose of 150 mg (range 100–250 mg), an average daily dose of 250 mg (range 200–300 mg/day) and a maximum daily dose of 750 mg. Doses up to 100 mg/day are taken once in the morning; doses over 100 mg/day are split into two administrations. Tablets are taken orally immediately after food. Typical treatment duration is two weeks to three months, averaging about 30 days, with a possible repeated course after one month. Administration after 15:00 is discouraged because of insomnia risk. [R15]

The same current source lists adult indications encompassing vascular/metabolic CNS disorders and chronic cerebrovascular insufficiency with impaired cognitive/motor function; neurotic states characterized by fatigue, reduced psychomotor activity, attention and memory problems; learning impairment; obesity; prevention of hypoxia/stress-related functional deterioration and fatigue; enhancement of wakefulness and mental/physical work capacity; and chronic alcoholism-associated asthenic/cognitive symptoms. These are approved-label claims, which are not equivalent to high-certainty randomized evidence for each indication. [R15]

“Phenotropil” is the historical brand attached to much of the older trial literature, and the stereochemical paper describes clinical Phenotropil as the racemate. “Actitropil” is a fonturacetam brand explicitly named in the 2025 asthenia meta-analysis. Brand names therefore do not identify a pure enantiomer. [R11][R1]

PK evidence requires unusually careful provenance. No modern primary human pharmacokinetic publication for racemic fonturacetam meeting the specified source policy was located, and no human PK paper for isolated R- or S-phenylpiracetam was found. The current Akrikhin patient-facing page captured for this review gives formulation, dosing and safety but does not display quantitative Cmax, Tmax, bioavailability, half-life, metabolic-clearance or renal-clearance data. Consequently, commonly repeated online figures for “100% bioavailability,” approximately one-hour Tmax, a several-hour half-life, or unchanged renal/biliary excretion should remain unverified label-provenance fields until the underlying approved professional SmPC is directly archived; they should not be presented as independently replicated human PK measurements. [R15]

## Safety, dependence and interactions

The 2025 meta-analysis reports an average adverse-event frequency of 5.5% at 200 mg/day and states that reported events were transient and resolved during continued treatment. Given the largely non-randomized/open nature of the evidence base feeding that synthesis, 5.5% should not be treated as a definitive population incidence or as evidence that higher doses and long-term use have comparable safety. [R1]

The current Russian Fonturacetam-Akrikhin leaflet lists the following adverse reactions with unknown frequency: insomnia when used after 15:00, psychomotor agitation, increased arterial blood pressure, skin hyperemia/flushing and a sensation of warmth. The latter four are described as having occurred in some patients during the first one to three days. The leaflet separately warns that somnolence can occur, particularly at treatment initiation, and advises avoiding driving or machinery if affected. [R15]

Cardiovascular risk: blood-pressure elevation is an explicitly labeled adverse event; the leaflet asks patients with severe hypertension or marked atherosclerosis to alert their physician. No dedicated human QT/QTc, arrhythmia, ambulatory-blood-pressure or long-term cardiovascular-outcomes study was located in the permitted evidence base. [R15]

Psychiatric risk: the current leaflet advises particular caution in patients with previous panic attacks or acute psychotic states accompanied by psychomotor agitation, noting the possibility of exacerbating anxiety, panic, hallucinations or delusions. Psychomotor agitation itself is listed as an adverse reaction. The 2014 cardiovascular/psychiatric study additionally found that phenotropil's apparent anxiolytic effect declined after four to eight weeks. [R15][R10]

Neurologic/seizure risk: the available epilepsy studies generally report fewer rather than more seizures when racemic phenotropil was used as add-on therapy, and one post-traumatic epilepsy report specifically noted absence of seizure aggravation. That does not establish the seizure threshold of high-dose or unsupervised use in healthy people, nor does it establish safety during abrupt discontinuation. [R6][R8]

Renal/hepatic impairment: the leaflet tells patients to disclose severe liver or kidney disease. No dedicated human renal-impairment PK study, hepatic-impairment PK study or evidence-based dose-adjustment algorithm was located. [R15]

Pregnancy and lactation: current labeling contraindicates fonturacetam during pregnancy and breastfeeding because data in pregnant and breastfeeding women are absent. This should be encoded as “no adequate human data/contraindicated by label,” not “proven teratogenic” or “proven safe.” [R15]

Pediatrics: the current leaflet contraindicates use from birth through age 17. [R15]

Interactions: the official leaflet specifically asks patients to report concomitant CNS stimulants, nootropics, antidepressants, neuroleptics, hexobarbital and ethanol. The captured patient leaflet does not provide quantitative exposure changes or interaction effect sizes. The epilepsy trials establish that phenotropil has been coadministered with antiseizure drugs, but those trials are not formal pharmacokinetic drug-interaction studies. [R15][R6]

Dependence, tolerance, withdrawal and misuse: no dedicated primary human abuse-liability, physical-dependence, tolerance or withdrawal trial was located. It would therefore be inappropriate to turn historical/label claims of “no dependence” into a high-confidence clinical conclusion. Preclinical DAT activity, stimulant-like labeling and WADA prohibition justify vigilance but do not quantify human addiction liability. [R12][R15][R17]

For overdose, the current leaflet states that fonturacetam overdose symptoms have not been described and recommends symptomatic management after excessive ingestion; that is a lack-of-data statement rather than reassurance about large-dose safety. [R15]

## Enantiomer-specific and preclinical mechanism evidence

This evidence should remain in a preclinical mechanism field and not be merged into human clinical outcomes.

The 2011 stereoselective study compared racemic phenotropil with its R- and S-enantiomers in mice. Brain concentrations of R and S were similar, yet behavioral activity differed: R-phenylpiracetam increased locomotion at lower tested doses than S, showed forced-swim activity at lower doses, and improved passive-avoidance memory at 1 mg/kg, whereas S did not show activity in that memory test. These are rodent behavioral findings and do not establish human nootropic potency. [R11]

MRZ-9547 is chemically identified in the Parkinson-model literature as the R-configured phenylpiracetam enantiomer. In a 2014 in-vitro/rat study, MRZ-9547 was a selective DAT inhibitor, was substantially more active than comparison enantiomer MRZ-9546, moderately increased striatal dopamine release and increased progressive-ratio effort responding. These results provide a plausible dopaminergic mechanism for R-phenylpiracetam but no human DAT-occupancy, abuse-liability, cardiovascular or cognition estimate. [R12]

A second MRZ-9547 paper examined rat models of Parkinsonism. MRZ-9547 had motor effects at 25–100 mg/kg, enhanced some effects of L-DOPA and did not significantly worsen established L-DOPA-induced abnormal involuntary movements at the reported test dose; microdialysis also found no rat pharmacokinetic interaction with L-DOPA. Those observations must not be converted into a claim of human Parkinson efficacy or a human L-DOPA interaction conclusion. [R13]

The picture is not simply “R active, S inactive.” A 2017 paper also characterizes S-phenylpiracetam as a selective DAT inhibitor and reports lower weight gain, fat accumulation, glucose and leptin abnormalities in rodent metabolic models without an increase in locomotor activity. Thus stereoselectivity appears endpoint- and assay-dependent, and no human R-versus-S trial was located. [R14]

## Regulatory status, bias and reference audit

Within the primary sources verified here, Russia has a current prescription fonturacetam medicine: Akrikhin's official page identifies Fonturacetam-Akrikhin as a 100-mg tablet and supplies current patient-label dosing, contraindications, adverse reactions and interaction precautions. Manufacturer labeling is appropriate evidence for approved formulation and safety wording, but because the manufacturer has an inherent commercial interest it should not be used by itself to establish clinical efficacy. [R15]

For sport, WADA's 2026 rules classify fonturacetam/4-phenylpiracetam/carphedon as an in-competition prohibited stimulant. [R17]

No FDA or EMA authorization record was used to support this report, so the record should avoid making an unverified blanket legal-status claim for the United States or European Union. Likewise, an online seller's use of “Phenotropil,” “phenylpiracetam,” “fonturacetam,” “R-phenylpiracetam” or “MRZ-9547” should not be treated as proof that its product is the registered Russian racemate, the pharmaceutical formulation studied clinically, or a chemically verified pure enantiomer.

The principal evidence-quality concern is not a complete absence of human data; it is that positive patient findings are concentrated in a small Russian-language literature with sparse methodological and disclosure reporting. Funding, formal sponsorship and COI information are absent from many PubMed records. Where the disclosure could not be directly verified, it is marked unknown, rather than silently interpreted as “no conflict.” [R2][R6][R13]

## Net BHWiki conclusion

Racemic fonturacetam has a real but methodologically weak-to-moderate Russian patient literature, with the most credible signals in asthenia/fatigue, stroke rehabilitation and add-on epilepsy treatment. The evidence is not transferable to isolated R-phenylpiracetam/MRZ-9547 or S-phenylpiracetam, whose DAT-related evidence remains preclinical. Healthy-person cognition and athletic-performance claims remain unconfirmed by posted human results as of October 10, 2026; NCT07852130 is completed but result-free. Safety labeling identifies insomnia, psychomotor excitation and blood-pressure elevation as salient risks, while dependence liability, formal human PK, organ-impairment PK, pregnancy/lactation safety, quantitative DDIs and long-term cardiovascular safety remain important evidence gaps. [R1][R15][R16]
