# Zeaxanthin — BHWiki Deep Research Record

Slug: zeaxanthin
Scope: standalone zeaxanthin; lutein, meso-zeaxanthin, AREDS2, foods, and multi-carotenoid products are kept explicitly separate.
Evidence cutoff: October 9, 2026.
Included evidence: primary human trials, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, official regulatory/safety assessments, pharmacovigilance sources, and clinical-trial registries only.

## Scope and evidence judgment

The standalone human evidence base is small and substantially weaker than the literature for lutein+zeaxanthin combinations. Oral zeaxanthin at roughly 8–20 mg/day has repeatedly increased circulating zeaxanthin and has increased macular-pigment biomarkers in several small studies, but not all of them. Those biomarker effects have not translated consistently into better clinical vision, and no robust standalone trial demonstrates prevention or slowed progression of AMD, treatment of MacTel2, slowing of axial/refractive myopia, relief of digital eye strain, or cognitive enhancement. [R1][R2][R3]

The central distinction for the BHWiki record should therefore be:

| Evidence class | What can reasonably be said |
|---|---|
| Serum/plasma zeaxanthin | Oral zeaxanthin is absorbed; exposure is materially formulation-dependent. |
| MPOD / retinal pigment | Standalone zeaxanthin can increase macular-pigment measures in some populations, but response is inconsistent and retinal spatial distribution matters. |
| Clinical visual function | Signals exist, but they are small, inconsistent, exploratory, or methodologically limited. |
| Disease modification | Not established for zeaxanthin alone. |
| Cognition / healthy enhancement | No published zeaxanthin-alone efficacy trial establishing benefit was located. |
| AREDS2 | Combination evidence only: lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg on a background AREDS formulation; never attribute its findings to zeaxanthin alone. [R4] |

## Macular pigment, vision and retinal disease

Macular pigment biomarkers. In LUXEA, randomized supplementation with lutein, zeaxanthin, or their combination at 10 or 20 mg/day produced large plasma xanthophyll increases. Lutein and L+Z increased MPOD by about 15%; zeaxanthin initially confounded conventional MPOD measurement because pigment appeared to accumulate similarly in foveal and parafoveal retina, but after correction the estimated zeaxanthin-associated increase was about 14%. The investigators interpreted the spatial pattern as broader retinal deposition of zeaxanthin compared with the more foveally concentrated lutein response. [R1]

ZEASTRESS-Pilot gave 20 mg/day standalone zeaxanthin for four months, followed by a four-month washout. MPOD at 0.5° and 2° rose significantly at supplementation end and remained increased during washout. Pattern electroretinogram amplitude improved at month four, whereas contrast sensitivity showed only a small, delayed and sex-dependent signal; dark-adapted foveal cone sensitivity and parafoveal rod sensitivity did not improve. Because this was a small pre/post pilot rather than a large placebo-controlled efficacy trial, it supports biologic activity more strongly than clinical benefit. [R2]

The biomarker literature is genuinely conflicting. In a randomized Japanese study of 22 healthy participants, 10 mg/day zeaxanthin for three months did not raise MPOD, either in high-myopic or non-high-myopic participants, whereas 10 mg/day lutein increased MPOD in non-high-myopic participants. [R3] This conflict is important because it argues against treating MPOD response as automatic at a given oral dose.

Visual performance. In the LUXEA-linked 2006 visual-performance study, supplementation groups showed a trend toward improved high-mesopic contrast acuity, but statistical significance was reported for the lutein group, not a clean zeaxanthin-alone comparison, and improvement did not correlate with MPOD. [R5] A companion color-vision analysis found MPOD increases but no improvement in yellow-blue discrimination and at most a marginal tendency toward better red-green discrimination. [R6] These are examples of retinal-biomarker change without a clear parallel functional effect.

AMD. The Zeaxanthin and Visual Function Study randomized 60 people with mild-to-moderate atrophic AMD to 8 mg zeaxanthin, 9 mg lutein, or 8 mg zeaxanthin + 9 mg lutein for one year. Zeaxanthin-alone participants showed an approximately 1.5-line high-contrast visual-acuity improvement, but MPOD rose across treatment groups without a significant between-group difference, while other functional endpoints were mixed or only trends. Critically, the nominal “placebo” was active lutein rather than an inert placebo; the trial was small, predominantly male, and evaluated multiple outcomes. It therefore does not establish that standalone zeaxanthin slows AMD progression. [R7] The registry identifies Chrysantis, Inc. as lead sponsor, with Kowa and Image Technologies as collaborators, making this an industry-sponsored study. [R8]

AREDS2 should remain quarantined from the standalone evidence. Its 4,203 high-risk AMD participants received lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg, DHA/EPA, both, or placebo while essentially all participants were offered an AREDS antioxidant/mineral background formula. The primary lutein+zeaxanthin add-on analysis did not significantly reduce progression to advanced AMD versus placebo: HR 0.90, 98.7% CI 0.76–1.07, P=0.12. [R4] The 2023 Cochrane update likewise characterized lutein/zeaxanthin evidence as low-certainty and compatible with little or no effect on late AMD, neovascular AMD, geographic atrophy, or ≥15-letter visual loss; its estimates are combination estimates dominated by AREDS2, not estimates of zeaxanthin alone. [R9]

Macular telangiectasia type 2. Eight MacTel2 participants received 10 or 20 mg/day zeaxanthin for up to 24 months. Serum zeaxanthin rose, and pigment increased in the preserved perifoveal ring, but supplementation did not refill the disease-characteristic central foveal pigment deficit. Best-corrected visual acuity and contrast sensitivity did not improve, and OCT did not show a therapeutic structural effect. [R10] This is particularly informative because it shows that increased systemic exposure and regional retinal uptake do not imply correction of disease-specific retinal pathology.

## Myopia, screen use and cognitive claims

For myopia, the main human intervention located that delivered substantial zeaxanthin used Lycium barbarum, providing approximately 10 mg zeaxanthin + 1 mg lutein or 20 mg zeaxanthin + 2 mg lutein, rather than purified zeaxanthin alone. The higher-dose food intervention increased MPOD relative to a low-dose lutein comparator after three months, but the intervention also contained lutein and a complex food matrix, and the study did not establish slowed axial elongation or refractive progression. It therefore belongs under combination/food evidence, not standalone zeaxanthin efficacy. [R11]

No eligible published randomized trial located in this search established that standalone zeaxanthin relieves digital eye strain. A pediatric randomized trial found that gummies containing 10 mg lutein + 2 mg zeaxanthin increased MPOD and reduced reported eye strain and fatigue, with selective cognitive improvements, but those effects cannot be assigned specifically to zeaxanthin. [R12]

The same attribution problem applies to cognition and healthy-person enhancement. Published positive studies predominantly use lutein+zeaxanthin or even broader multi-carotenoid preparations and commonly contain null cognitive domains alongside positive ones. No published zeaxanthin-alone randomized efficacy study establishing a nootropic effect was located. A particularly relevant completed registry trial, NCT02017418, randomized 102 healthy adults to a zeaxanthin softgel versus a zeaxanthin+lutein+DHA formulation and registered MPOD and visuomotor/reaction-time endpoints; however, the registry has no posted results, and no PubMed article was located by the NCT identifier. University of Georgia was lead sponsor and ZeaVision was a collaborator. [R13] This unpublished registry record should increase, rather than decrease, caution about publication bias in healthy-performance claims.

## Dose, formulation and pharmacokinetics

Human standalone doses actually studied include 8 mg/day for one year in ZVF; 10 mg/day in the Japanese MPOD study and the lower MacTel2 arm; and 20 mg/day in ZEASTRESS-Pilot, LUXEA's higher-dose phase, and the higher MacTel2 arm. [R1][R3][R7][R10]

A current phase-1 metastatic-cancer study is testing much higher oral exposures: 2, 4, 6 and 8 mg/kg/day, with zeaxanthin supplied as 50-mg capsules, both as monotherapy and alongside pembrolizumab. Its objectives include maximum tolerated dose, dose-limiting toxicity, Cmax and AUC. The registry was still recruiting at its June 5, 2026 update and had no posted results, so these doses should not be treated as established safe doses for supplement use. [R14]

Formulation materially alters serum exposure. In a randomized single-dose study of 90 healthy men, both products delivered the same 10 mg lutein + 2 mg 3R,3′R-zeaxanthin dose, but an oil-based, micronized Ocusorb formulation containing MCT and other excipients produced approximately 1.75-fold greater zeaxanthin Cmax and 2.2-fold greater AUC than the reference formulation. No adverse events were reported. This is a serum PK finding, not evidence that the formulation produces 2.2-fold greater retinal accumulation or clinical benefit. [R15]

That PK paper also has an unusually important commercial-context flag: OmniActive Health Technologies funded the study and publication support, supplied the investigational products, and several authors were OmniActive employees. The paper stated that the authors had no known competing interests, but for BHWiki purposes the combination of sponsor funding, product ownership and author employment should be classified as a material commercial conflict, not as an unconflicted efficacy demonstration. [R15]

## Safety, pregnancy, organ impairment and interactions

Short standalone eye trials generally did not reveal common systemic toxicity, but their small sizes and limited durations mean they cannot exclude uncommon or delayed harms. The most important zeaxanthin-specific ocular adverse observation was in MacTel2: one participant receiving 20 mg/day developed yellowish, hypofluorescent inner-retinal crystalline deposits after several months. No corresponding loss of visual acuity or scotoma was reported; supplementation was stopped, the deposits disappeared, and the participant later resumed 10 mg/day. [R10] A single reversible case does not establish an incidence rate, but it is sufficiently specific to merit inclusion under “retinal crystals/crystalline maculopathy.”

There is no dedicated human standalone-zeaxanthin trial in significant renal or hepatic impairment among the eligible evidence located. The ongoing high-dose oncology study requires adequate marrow, bilirubin, AST/ALT and creatinine values at enrollment, so its eventual findings will not automatically generalize to patients with clinically important organ dysfunction. [R14]

Pregnancy evidence is combination-only. L-ZIP randomized 47 low-risk pregnant participants to a standard prenatal vitamin plus 10 mg lutein + 2 mg zeaxanthin in safflower oil or matched safflower-oil placebo for approximately six to eight months. Supplementation substantially increased maternal serum/skin carotenoid biomarkers and infant cord-blood/skin carotenoid status. Reported adverse events did not differ appreciably between groups; investigators judged two severe infant events in the supplemented group to have originated before enrollment and to be unrelated to study treatment. [R16] The trial was funded by NIH grants EY029857/EY014800 and Research to Prevent Blindness, Kemin supplied the intervention, and the authors declared no conflicts of interest.

L-ZIP does not establish lactation safety: investigators explicitly reported that funding and logistical limitations prevented follow-up beyond the early postpartum visit, so breastmilk transfer and continued postnatal supplementation were not studied. [R16]

No controlled published human trial establishing a clinically important zeaxanthin–medication interaction was located. The current oncology phase-1 trial includes a zeaxanthin+pembrolizumab arm, but it has no posted results; therefore the registry shows only that this combination is being studied, not that it is safe, harmful, synergistic or pharmacokinetically neutral. [R14]

## Regulatory status, sponsorship and reference record

EFSA's 2012 reassessment derived a zeaxanthin safe intake of 0.75 mg/kg body weight/day, about 53 mg/day for a 70-kg adult, from a two-generation rat NOAEL of 150 mg/kg/day with a 200-fold uncertainty factor. This is a toxicologic risk-assessment level, not an efficacy dose and not the same thing as an authorized supplement dose. The preceding 2008 assessment had found the evidence insufficient to establish safety at the applicant's proposed 20 mg/day; additional toxicology subsequently changed EFSA's assessment. [R17]

EU novel-food authorization was granted at the applicant-requested maximum manufacturer-recommended intake of 2 mg/day. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/1132 subsequently retained the use conditions while changing the required designation from “synthetic zeaxanthin” to “zeaxanthin.” The official regulation explicitly notes that the earlier 2-mg/day authorization remained unchanged; it should not be misrepresented as an EFSA finding that 2 mg/day is a toxicity threshold. [R18]

In the United States, FDA's official GRAS database records GRN 588 for paprika-derived zeaxanthin in specified conventional foods at approximately 0.3–3 mg per serving, submitted by OmniActive, and GRN 639 for marigold-derived zeaxanthin at up to 300 μg per serving in specified foods including baby/toddler foods, submitted by Kemin. In both cases FDA responded that it had “no questions,” while noting that some intended uses could require color-additive authorization. A GRAS no-questions response concerns specified food uses; it is not FDA approval of zeaxanthin as a drug or endorsement of an AMD, cognition, myopia, or digital-eye-strain claim. [R19]

No public zeaxanthin-specific causal pharmacovigilance signal assessment was identified in the official regulatory sources reviewed. That absence should not be converted into “no adverse events”: food-supplement reporting is incomplete and jurisdiction-dependent; for example, Switzerland explicitly states that it does not operate a dedicated nutrivigilance system and instead reports food-supplement poisoning enquiries in aggregate. [R20]

Commercial involvement is common enough to be a standing evidence-quality consideration. ZVF was industry-sponsored by Chrysantis with Kowa/Image Technologies collaborators; the LUXEA program had DSM product/funding links; MacTel2 received public/philanthropic support but ZeaVision supplied product; the healthy visuomotor registry lists ZeaVision as collaborator; and the Ocusorb PK study was directly OmniActive-funded with sponsor employees among the authors. Conversely, AREDS2 was led by the National Eye Institute with other NIH components, and L-ZIP was NIH/Research to Prevent Blindness funded with Kemin providing product. [R8][R1][R10][R15][R16]
