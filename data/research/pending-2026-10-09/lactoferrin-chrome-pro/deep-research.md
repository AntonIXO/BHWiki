# Lactoferrin — BHWiki Deep Research

Slug: lactoferrin
Evidence cutoff: October 10, 2026
Scope: primary human trials, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, official regulatory/safety sources, clinical-trial registries, and official pharmacovigilance infrastructure only. Bovine, human/recombinant, apo/low-saturation, holo/iron-saturated, oral, intranasal/topical, and combination products are treated as distinct interventions.

## Evidence position

Lactoferrin should not be represented as a single interchangeable therapeutic entity. The human literature contains materially different products: milk-derived bovine lactoferrin (bLF), recombinant human lactoferrin (rhLF), recombinant bovine lactoferrin, preparations with very low versus partial/high iron saturation, and combination products containing therapeutic iron, infant formula, probiotics, antibiotics, or other drugs. Route also matters: oral/enteral evidence cannot be extrapolated to intranasal or vaginal administration. [R1][R2]

The strongest modern adult IDA evidence is negative for stand-alone unsaturated/apo bLF as an iron substitute. In the 2026 randomized Bangladeshi trial, 555 nonpregnant women with IDA received 200 mg bLF/day, 400 mg bLF/day, or 60 mg elemental iron/day as ferrous sulfate, each with folic acid for 12 weeks. The bLF was an unsaturated apo preparation providing only about 0.026 or 0.051 mg elemental iron/day. Hemoglobin changed by −0.2, 0.0, and +1.1 g/dL, respectively; both bLF doses were inferior to ferrous sulfate. Ferritin likewise improved strongly with ferrous sulfate but minimally with bLF. Adverse-event frequencies were broadly comparable. [R1]

This result is especially important because it conflicts with earlier pregnancy and anemia literature favoring bLF. Older evidence is methodologically weaker, and one influential pregnancy IDA randomized trial was subsequently retracted in 2023. [R3]

Human oral evidence also does not justify assuming clinically significant intact-protein systemic exposure. In eight women with ileostomies who swallowed 5 g recombinant human LF, only a median 4 micrograms of intact LF was recovered from 24-hour ileal output; investigators concluded that ingested rhLF was digested in the upper GI tract and that the small intact signal was probably endogenous. [R4] This makes it inappropriate to infer systemic antimicrobial, antiviral, immunomodulatory, or disease-modifying effects merely from in-vitro mechanisms.

## Iron deficiency and hematologic outcomes

### Pregnancy and nonpregnant adults

Older pregnancy studies generally used bLF 100 mg twice daily, sometimes explicitly about 30% iron saturated, against very high nominal doses of ferrous sulfate. In the 2006 Paesano controlled trial, 300 pregnant women received either 30%-iron-saturated bLF 100 mg twice daily, ferrous sulfate 520 mg once daily, or—among women refusing treatment—no treatment for 30 days. Hemoglobin and serum iron increased in the bLF group, and no bLF adverse effects were reported. The untreated group was not randomized, so the result is substantially less secure than a blinded randomized comparison. [R5]

A later pregnancy meta-analysis reported a pooled hemoglobin advantage for lactoferrin over ferrous salts but no convincing superiority for ferritin, serum iron, or obstetric outcomes. Its interpretation now requires particular caution because the evidence set contained the subsequently retracted Rezk pregnancy trial; the retraction means historical pooled estimates should not be treated as clean confirmation of efficacy. [R3]

The 2026 Huda trial is a major counterweight to that older literature. Besides the negative hemoglobin result, it found that ferritin increased by only approximately 3 micrograms/L on either apo-bLF dose versus roughly 41 micrograms/L with ferrous sulfate. Hepcidin rose much less on bLF, but CRP and AGP did not show a clear systemic anti-inflammatory advantage and the lower hepcidin response did not translate into hematologic efficacy. The authors explicitly concluded that unsaturated bLF should not routinely replace ferrous sulfate in similar women with IDA. Funding came from Saving Lives at Birth and the UK Medical Research Council; the funders were reported to have no role in study conduct or reporting, and the authors declared no relevant conflicts. [R1]

### Children and IBD

The most directly relevant pediatric IBD trial randomized children aged 5–18 years with IBD in remission and IDA to bLF 100 mg/day as Pravotin sachets or ferrous sulfate 6 mg/kg/day for three months. Eighty children completed treatment. Both groups improved hematologic indices, but the bLF group had higher post-treatment hemoglobin, serum iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin; IL-6 and hepcidin fell in the bLF arm. Gastrointestinal adverse effects were reported much more frequently with ferrous sulfate. [R6]

That result should remain classified as promising but low-replication evidence, not a settled replacement strategy. It was a relatively small single-center study, excluded children with chronic renal or liver disease and active infection, and therefore does not establish efficacy or safety in those populations. The article also contains some internally awkward adverse-event reporting: some ferrous-sulfate participants were excluded after discontinuation associated with adverse effects, while another passage states that none of the reported side effects necessitated discontinuation. [R6]

Combination-product evidence should be kept separate. A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of controlled pediatric trials comparing iron + bLF versus iron alone found only a modest additional ferritin increase of 3.52 micrograms/L (95% CI 0.66–6.38); hemoglobin, serum iron, and soluble transferrin receptor were not significantly improved. Thus these studies support, at most, a possible effect on iron storage rather than demonstrated superior correction of anemia. [R7]

Trials of bLF-fortified infant formula are similarly not equivalent to isolated lactoferrin therapy. For example, infant studies used bLF in iron-fortified formula, so favorable hematologic or infectious outcomes cannot be attributed to lactoferrin as an iron substitute without qualification. [R8]

## Infection and gastrointestinal outcomes

The infection literature contains a clear small-trial versus large-trial conflict. The pivotal ELFIN trial randomized 2,203 very preterm infants to enteral milk-derived bLF 150 mg/kg/day, maximum 300 mg/day, versus sucrose placebo until 34 weeks' postmenstrual age. Late-onset infection occurred in 316/1,093 infants receiving bLF and 334/1,089 controls, adjusted RR 0.95 (95% CI 0.86–1.04; p=0.233). There was also no meaningful benefit for microbiologically confirmed infection, mortality, necrotizing enterocolitis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, antimicrobial days, or hospital stay. [R9]

ELFIN therefore contradicted the much larger effects suggested by earlier small neonatal trials. Sixteen serious adverse events occurred in the lactoferrin arm versus ten in controls; blood in stool and a death after intestinal perforation were assessed as possibly related to the intervention. The trial was funded by the UK NIHR Health Technology Assessment program, with no funder role in study design, collection, analysis, interpretation, or manuscript preparation. [R9]

Systematic reviews consequently look more favorable than the largest individual trial because they combine ELFIN with earlier small studies. This should be reflected explicitly in a BHWiki evidence grade: pooled neonatal-sepsis estimates are not equivalent to a replicated large-trial effect.

In weaned infants, a randomized study of bLF-fortified formula reported fewer diarrhea and respiratory illnesses over three months than formula without added bLF. The intervention contained 38 mg bLF/100 g formula and was an iron-fortified formula product; breastfed infants were an additional reference group. This finding supports a possible formula-level effect in that population but not oral bLF monotherapy for acute diarrhea or respiratory infection. [R8]

For respiratory infections more broadly, a 2021 RCT meta-analysis estimated reduced odds of respiratory infection with lactoferrin, pooled OR 0.57 (95% CI 0.44–0.74; n=1,194), while noting high risk of bias in two included studies. [R10] More recent pediatric synthesis has been less convincing for upper respiratory infection specifically, so a broad “prevents respiratory infections” claim would overstate consistency.

For H. pylori, older randomized-trial meta-analyses suggested higher eradication rates when lactoferrin was added to standard antibiotic/PPI eradication regimens, sometimes with fewer treatment adverse effects. This is evidence about an adjunctive combination product/regimen—not lactoferrin monotherapy and not proof that lactoferrin itself eradicates H. pylori.

Systemic antiviral claims have not translated reliably. In the double-blind LAC trial, 218 hospitalized adults with moderate-to-severe COVID-19 received 800 mg/day oral bLF, formulated as 200-mg Mosiac capsules, approximately 12% iron saturated, or placebo in addition to standard care. There was no benefit for the death/ICU composite, discharge or NEWS2 improvement, oxygen/ventilation outcomes, CRP, IL-6, or ferritin; adverse-event proportions did not differ materially. [R2] Oral bLF 1.8 g/day for 12 weeks likewise failed to improve HCV virologic response or ALT versus placebo in a 198-patient chronic hepatitis C trial. [R11]

These clinical null results are a strong reason not to convert in-vitro “antiviral” or “immunomodulatory” mechanisms into disease-benefit statements.

## Bioavailability, routes, and interactions

Human gastric data show that iron saturation changes digestive stability. In the adult gastric-digestion experiment, volunteers received 4.5 g bLF under three conditions: buffered nominal apo/low-saturation bLF, unbuffered nominal apo/low-saturation bLF, and iron-saturated holo-bLF. Approximate gastric survival was 64%, 62%, and 79%, respectively. That establishes greater gastric stability of holo-lactoferrin, not greater systemic exposure or superior clinical efficacy. [R4]

Recombinant human LF behaves differently and should not be pooled unquestioningly with bovine material. The 5-g ileostomy study demonstrated extensive upper-GI digestion and essentially no recovery of the administered dose as intact protein at the terminal small bowel. [R4]

A newer healthy-adult study tested Helaina recombinant human LF produced in Komagataella phaffii at 0.34 or 3.4 g/day for 28 days against bLF 3.4 g/day. No treatment-emergent anti-human-LF antibody response was detected with either rhLF dose and routine safety outcomes remained broadly similar between groups. By contrast, anti-bLF antibodies increased in the bLF group; the clinical significance is unknown. [R12] Three low-iron-saturation adverse events—one with bLF and two with low-dose rhLF—were classified as possibly or probably related, although baseline iron saturation was already abnormal in those participants. [R13]

That rhLF study has an important sponsorship caveat: Helaina funded it and participated in study design, data interpretation, manuscript writing, and the decision to submit; several authors were Helaina employees/equity holders, another received Helaina consulting/advisory fees, and contract-research authors disclosed Helaina and other industry relationships. [R13]

Evidence for intranasal administration is much weaker. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04475120 tested a low-iron-saturation liposomal bLF formulation using approximately 1 g/day orally plus an ~8 mg/mL intranasal spray three times daily in a small COVID-19 program. Because of its open/pilot design and mixed comparator groups, it should be kept separate from oral randomized evidence and does not establish an intranasal antiviral indication.

No qualifying human formal pharmacokinetic drug-drug-interaction study was identified in this evidence set. The rhLF safety trial actually excluded several drugs that could confound absorption or outcomes—such as PPIs, H2 blockers, antacids, anti-inflammatory drugs, laxatives and some supplements—so that study cannot establish compatibility or interaction effects with them. [R12]

For iron supplementation, the best current distinction is: lactoferrin plus iron may modestly alter ferritin in pediatric combination studies, whereas unsaturated bLF without a therapeutically meaningful iron dose failed as a replacement for elemental iron in adult IDA. [R7][R1]

## Safety and special populations

Short-term oral bLF has generally been well tolerated in human trials, and older anemia comparisons frequently report fewer constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, or other GI effects than high-dose ferrous sulfate. That tolerability advantage must not be confused with superior anemia efficacy: the strongest adult IDA trial found similar overall adverse-event rates but markedly superior hematologic efficacy with ferrous sulfate. [R1]

Pregnancy has direct human exposure data, but most studies are old, short, small, and focused on laboratory iron indices rather than hard maternal or neonatal outcomes. One important pregnancy efficacy trial is retracted. [R5][R3] Dedicated lactation pharmacokinetic or infant-safety evidence was not identified in the qualifying evidence reviewed; modern studies commonly excluded lactating women. Pregnancy and lactation should therefore have separate BHWiki evidence fields.

Renal and hepatic impairment likewise remain evidence gaps rather than established contraindications or dose-adjustment indications. The pediatric IBD study excluded chronic renal and hepatic disease, while the LAC COVID trial excluded end-stage renal disease. [R6][R2] The chronic-HCV trial provides some exposure experience in liver disease but showed neither virologic nor ALT benefit. [R11] A registered diabetic-nephropathy trial remains investigational rather than evidence of renal benefit.

Passive pharmacovigilance cannot presently provide a defensible lactoferrin adverse-event incidence from the material reviewed. A lack of a quantified signal should not be represented as evidence that serious or rare adverse reactions do not occur. Controlled-trial denominators and official food-safety assessments are more interpretable for the present record.

## Regulatory and conflict assessment

In the United States, the clearest official regulatory status located is food-ingredient GRAS status, not therapeutic approval. FDA GRN 464 covers cow's-milk-derived lactoferrin in specified foods; GRN 465 covers specified concentrations in cow's-milk-based term infant formulas. Both were closed in 2014 with FDA “has no questions” letters. [R14][R15] These determinations should not be described as FDA approval for treating IDA, infection, IBD, COVID-19, or another disease.

FDA has more recently closed “no questions” notices for recombinant bovine lactoferrin made in K. phaffii: GRN 1219, submitted by TurtleTree and closed May 7, 2025, and GRN 1284, submitted by All G Co Holdings and closed March 25, 2026. Their intended uses are food applications, not disease indications. [R16][R17]

Historical FDA notices for recombinant/purified human lactoferrin are different: the human-LF GRAS notices located in the FDA inventory were ceased at the notifier's request rather than receiving the same completed “no questions” outcome. [R18][R19] This distinction should be preserved rather than treating “lactoferrin is GRAS” as a source-independent statement.

EFSA's official 2012 opinion concluded that bovine lactoferrin was safe under the proposed novel-food uses and intake levels, including estimated high adult intake around 3.4 g/day and infant exposure around 200–210 mg/kg/day. The opinion was a food-safety assessment, drawing substantially on toxicology and submitted safety information; it is not evidence of therapeutic efficacy. [R20]

The sponsorship pattern is relevant to evidence grading. Some of the strongest newer null evidence is publicly funded: the 2026 adult IDA trial was funded by Saving Lives at Birth and the UK Medical Research Council and declared no author conflicts, while ELFIN was funded by UK NIHR. [R1][R9] Conversely, recombinant-product development includes direct commercial participation: the 2025 Helaina rhLF safety trial was company-funded with employees/equity holders among the authors and sponsor involvement in design and publication. [R13] Older positive pregnancy and formula studies frequently have incomplete funding or COI reporting in the accessible records; those disclosures are marked unknown, rather than being treated as conflict-free.
