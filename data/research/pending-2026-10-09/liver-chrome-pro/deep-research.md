## Record scope and bottom line

This record treats cooked whole liver, raw/undercooked liver, processed liver foods, beef/calf/chicken liver, desiccated liver powder/capsules, and multi-ingredient liver/tissue products as distinct exposures. Animal liver is a heterogeneous food matrix containing variable amounts of preformed vitamin A, vitamin B12, folate, total iron, copper, zinc, protein, cholesterol and other constituents. Species, cooking, water loss, formulation dilution, fortification and drying can change exposure substantially. USDA composition records show large differences among raw beef, cooked beef, calf/veal, chicken liver and liver sausage. [R1]

There is no defensible single “active molecule,” parent-compound pharmacokinetics, oral bioavailability, Tmax, elimination route, or elimination half-life for liver. Vitamin A, B12, folate, iron, copper and zinc each have different absorption, transport, storage and biomarker behavior; a liver food or powder cannot inherit the PK of one constituent. NIH nutrient guidance treats these as separate nutrients rather than as a pharmacologic “liver” entity. [R2]

The direct human evidence is narrow. Historical pernicious-anemia treatment used a multi-food diet containing roughly 120–240 g/day of cooked calf or beef liver plus about 120 g/day of muscle meat, rather than a modern liver capsule. Modern evidence includes a small chicken-liver-sausage anemia study, an acceptability study of desiccated beef-liver/meat powders in young children, and the SISTIK chicken-liver/eggshell cracker program in pregnancy. These formulations and doses are not interchangeable. [R3,R4,R5,R6]

The strongest directly relevant modern randomized evidence identified is SISTIK. It did not improve the reported maternal hemoglobin/anemia, birth or infant linear-growth outcomes, or morbidity versus control. Interpretation is complicated by low adherence and substantial concurrent iron-folic-acid/micronutrient exposure. [R6]

Conversely, clinically important safety concerns are well grounded: unusually high preformed vitamin A in whole liver, unusually high copper in beef liver, raw/undercooked chicken-liver infection risk, and additional iron exposure where iron overload is already a concern. These risks depend strongly on species, amount and formulation. [R2,R7,R8,R9]

## Composition, formulation, and nutrient handling

### Whole liver and species variability

A checked official-composition anchor illustrates the concentration problem: approximately 3 oz/85 g of pan-fried beef liver provides about 6,582 µg RAE of vitamin A, roughly 12.4 mg copper, approximately 70.7 µg B12, and around 5 mg total iron in NIH/USDA tables. The adult tolerable upper intake level for preformed vitamin A is 3,000 µg RAE/day and the adult copper UL is 10 mg/day, so a normal beef-liver serving can exceed each benchmark. UL comparisons are safety benchmarks, not evidence that a single isolated serving necessarily causes toxicity. [R1,R2]

Species and processing cannot be collapsed into a generic “liver” value. USDA SR-Legacy B12 entries include approximately 50.4 µg per 3 oz raw beef liver, 48.0 µg per 68 g slice of braised beef liver, 17.0 µg per 1 oz raw veal liver, 48.6 µg per 67 g cooked/pan-fried veal liver, 9.3 µg per 44 g cooked chicken liver, and about 16.0 µg per 3 oz liver sausage. These values show species and processing/serving-size variability; they are composition measurements, not clinical-effect doses. [R1]

Liver also supplies folate and zinc, but absolute ratios vary by species and preparation. Food-composition records should therefore remain item-level rather than replacing beef, calf and chicken liver with one averaged nutrient profile.

Total iron should not be relabeled “heme iron.” Official food-composition databases report total iron. Animal tissues contain heme iron, but hepatic iron also includes non-heme storage pools; a listing of “5 mg iron” does not establish a 5-mg heme-iron exposure. Iron absorption also depends on iron status and the meal matrix. Hemoglobin, ferritin, transferrin saturation and soluble transferrin receptor are appropriate status variables rather than assuming biological effect from food iron content alone. [R2]

### SISTIK demonstrates why multi-ingredient liver foods need their own record

The SISTIK intervention was 75 g/day of a deep-fried cracker, not 75 g/day of chicken liver. The product contained chicken liver and powdered eggshell plus wheat flour, tapioca, egg, margarine, mushroom bouillon, salt and seasoning. [R6]

The final trial publication reported the following average content per nominal 75-g MEC serving:

| Nutrient/exposure | MEC chicken-liver/eggshell cracker | Wheat-cracker control |
|---|---:|---:|
| Energy | 420.5 kcal | 432.2 kcal |
| Retinol | 161.3 µg RAE | 66.1 µg RAE |
| Vitamin B12 | 0.77 µg | 0.07 µg |
| Folate | 49.6 µg | 44.6 µg |
| Calcium | 506.0 mg | 74.9 mg |
| Total iron | 4.0 mg | 4.2 mg |
| Zinc | 2.7 mg | 2.2 mg |

The chicken-liver product actually contained slightly less total iron than its control; fortified wheat flour was a substantial source of control-group micronutrients. It is invalid to interpret SISTIK as a clean test of “chicken-liver iron.” [R6]

The protocol had earlier characterized the nominal 75-g MEC differently—approximately 314 µg RAE vitamin A, 2.3 µg B12, 74.5 µg folate, 554 mg calcium, 3.6 mg iron and 4.4 mg zinc. The difference between development-stage and trial-stage values is a real-world warning against assuming that a recipe, ingredient list or one early laboratory result permanently defines a processed liver product. [R5,R6]

## Absorption and measured biomarkers

Vitamin B12 is one example of genuinely nutrient-specific absorption. Okuda, Takara and Fujii directly studied liver-bound B12 in relation to intrinsic factor in humans in 1968. That does not produce a PK model for liver; it demonstrates that food-bound cobalamin remains subject to intrinsic-factor-dependent absorption. [R10]

For iron, hematologic outcomes and status markers need to remain separate: hemoglobin is not synonymous with ferritin, and ferritin can rise with inflammation or liver disease. SISTIK prospectively registered ferritin, soluble transferrin receptor, RBP, zinc and selenium with inflammatory adjustment, plus breast-milk micronutrients, but the 2025 primary publication reported principal growth, Hb/anemia and morbidity findings rather than the entire registered micronutrient-biomarker panel. Unreported registered outcomes should not be silently coded as null. [R6]

The same distinction applies to vitamin A, folate, copper and zinc: nutrient-specific laboratory status is not equivalent to “liver absorption,” and neither a high food-content value nor a biomarker change by itself proves a functional benefit.

## Human evidence by outcome

### Pernicious anemia

Minot and Murphy's landmark 1926 intervention showed striking hematologic recovery in patients with pernicious anemia before vitamin B12 had been isolated. The intervention was a special high-protein, multi-food diet. Historical descriptions preserve the exposure as approximately 120–240 g/day of cooked calf or beef liver plus about 120 g/day muscle meat, with reticulocyte responses appearing within days in responders. [R3]

This establishes historical efficacy of a liver-containing dietary regimen in untreated pernicious anemia; it does not establish that ordinary servings, desiccated-liver capsules, or liver in B12-replete people increase energy, exercise performance or cognition. The later human literature on liver-bound B12 and intrinsic factor reinforces the disease-specific nature of the historical response. [R10]

### Modern anemia and chicken-liver sausage

A 2025 Indonesian quasi-experimental study in anemic adolescent girls evaluated an F6 chicken-liver sausage for four weeks. The intervention produced a significant change in the study's reported iron-related laboratory outcome, but hemoglobin did not significantly improve in the intervention group, and the between-group Hb change was not significant; the control group had a significant within-group Hb change. This is mixed, low-certainty evidence rather than a confirmed anti-anemia effect. [R11]

The exact daily gram serving could not be recovered from the checked accessible source. It remains unverified rather than reconstructed from recipe assumptions.

### Pregnancy and maternal/infant outcomes

SISTIK randomized 28 Indonesian village clusters, enrolling 324 women; 137 mother-infant dyads in each group completed follow-up. Women were assigned to nominally consume 75 g/day, or 525 g/week, of chicken-liver/eggshell MEC or control cracker from early pregnancy through five months postpartum. Both groups also received nutrition/health interventions and access to national supplementation programs. [R6]

Actual exposure was far lower. Median MEC consumption was about 103 g/week during pregnancy and 72 g/week postpartum, versus intended 525 g/week. Approximately 81% consumed at least 90 tablets of iron/folic acid and/or multiple micronutrients during pregnancy, and about 69% consumed at least one calcium tablet. [R6]

The trial found no significant intervention benefit on growth outcomes. Late-pregnancy maternal Hb was approximately 12.0 versus 12.3 g/dL in MEC versus control, and anemia prevalence was 11.9% versus 11.3%; these were not intervention benefits. Morbidity likewise did not differ materially. [R6]

The null result does not prove that liver has no nutrient effects: actual exposure was low, the food was highly diluted relative to whole liver, the control contained fortified flour, and concomitant supplements were common. Equally, those limitations cannot turn a null clinical trial into efficacy evidence. SISTIK cannot be cited as evidence that whole liver is safe during pregnancy: the final cracker contained only 161.3 µg RAE retinol per nominal 75-g serving and actual adherence was low, while a typical whole-beef-liver serving supplies several milligrams RAE. [R1,R6]

### Pediatric evidence

The 2017 Indonesian study of desiccated beef liver powder, beef meat powder, liver+meat powder and placebo enrolled 96 children aged 12–23 months. It evaluated liking and feasibility through test feeding and a two-week home trial. Maternal ratings were generally acceptable, but powder disappearance was low, and a strong/fishy odor was an important reason children rejected meals; the liver+meat combination was less liked in some comparisons. No anemia, ferritin, B12-status, cognition, growth or recovery efficacy follows from this acceptability study. [R4]

The exact powder gram dose was not recoverable from the checked PubMed record and is intentionally left unfilled.

### Energy, recovery, cognition, liver disease, and “detox”

No qualifying modern published controlled human evidence established that stand-alone whole liver or desiccated-liver capsules improve energy, exercise recovery, cognition, “detoxification,” or established liver disease independently of correcting a specific deficiency.

An old 1964 paper used a composite “liver antitoxic fraction, liver extract-aspartates-yeast” preparation; it is neither liver-only nor sufficiently documented to support contemporary efficacy claims.

NCT07256639 studies Heart & Soil's “Whole Package,” explicitly described as a combination of beef liver, testicle and whole-blood extract. It is an open-label, single-group before/after study in an estimated 100 adult men measuring testosterone-related blood tests and the self-reported Aging Male Symptoms scale. The registry was last updated May 28, 2026 and had no posted results; it must remain multi-tissue, commercial-product, unreported evidence, not evidence for liver capsules generally. [R12]

Subjective reports of energy, libido, vitality or recovery must remain separate from measured hemoglobin, ferritin, B12, retinol/RBP or hormone outcomes. Anecdotes are not efficacy data.

## Safety and special populations

### Preformed vitamin A, toxicity, and pregnancy

Whole liver is unusual among ordinary foods because of its concentration of preformed vitamin A. NIH sets the adult UL for preformed vitamin A at 3,000 µg RAE/day; the official beef-liver value around 6,582 µg RAE per 3 oz is more than twice that benchmark. The relevant hazard is cumulative/high retinol exposure, not a toxic “liver molecule.” [R1,R2]

The major prospective human pregnancy study by Rothman and colleagues followed 22,748 pregnancies. For cranial-neural-crest-associated defects, women consuming more than 15,000 IU/day of preformed vitamin A from food and supplements had a prevalence ratio of 3.5 versus women consuming 5,000 IU/day or less; for supplements alone, more than 10,000 IU/day yielded a prevalence ratio of 4.8 versus ≤5,000 IU/day. The excess was concentrated in exposure before the seventh gestational week. This was observational and not liver-specific, so it informs total-retinol risk rather than being a trial of liver. [R13]

UK NHS guidance explicitly advises avoiding liver and liver products during pregnancy because of vitamin-A content and cautions that people eating liver regularly should not simultaneously take vitamin-A-containing supplements.

### Copper burden and Wilson disease

Approximately 12.4 mg copper per 3 oz pan-fried beef liver exceeds the adult UL of 10 mg/day. A UL is a chronic population safety benchmark, not a diagnostic threshold or proof of poisoning from one meal. [R1,R2]

For Wilson disease, NIDDK advises limiting/avoiding high-copper foods including liver, particularly early in treatment. A desiccated liver capsule should not be assumed low-copper merely because serving mass is small; without a product assay, capsule-to-copper conversion is uncertain. [R14]

### Iron overload and hemochromatosis

Liver contributes total iron, but food-composition iron is not a direct measure of absorbed iron. In suspected or established iron overload, ferritin and complementary iron studies are clinically relevant, and ferritin can also be elevated by inflammation and liver disease. No liver-specific clinical safe dose for hereditary hemochromatosis was identified. The appropriate handling is caution rather than an invented threshold. Concurrent liver plus medicinal or supplemental iron increases total iron exposure and should be evaluated with measured iron status.

### Raw and undercooked liver

The human infection evidence is strong for chicken liver. A 2011 East-of-England outbreak involved 49 cases and showed a dose-response association between chicken-liver pâté consumption and Campylobacter illness: risk rose as amount eaten increased. [R15]

A U.S. multistate investigation linked six Campylobacter cases to raw or lightly cooked chicken livers and isolated the outbreak strain from livers at the implicated establishment; CDC concluded that full cooking is necessary because chicken livers can be contaminated. [R16]

Another UK cohort documented a dual Campylobacter/Salmonella outbreak associated with chicken-liver pâté. Multiple additional outbreak investigations and microbiological studies reinforce the same point: a seared exterior with an undercooked center is not equivalent to a validated kill step. [R17]

Any supposed nutritional advantage of raw liver must not be inferred from nutrient-content arguments while ignoring a demonstrated human infectious-disease risk. USDA-FSIS guidance favors thorough cooking of poultry liver and liver-containing preparations.

### Renal/hepatic impairment and concurrent medications

No eligible direct study establishes a generic liver-food or desiccated-liver dose adjustment for chronic kidney disease or nonspecific hepatic impairment. Nor can such an adjustment be calculated from a nonexistent liver half-life. Disease-specific management must follow the relevant nutrient burden and laboratory status—for example copper in Wilson disease and iron in iron-overload disorders.

Claims that liver “detoxifies” a damaged liver are unsupported by the direct human evidence located.

A current U.S. isotretinoin label states that vitamin-A supplements should be avoided concomitantly with isotretinoin because isotretinoin is closely related to vitamin A and additive adverse reactions may occur. The label does not specifically name liver food, so it should not be rewritten as an official food contraindication; high-retinol liver intake nevertheless contributes to total preformed-retinoid exposure. [R18]

The same accounting principle applies to concurrent retinol, iron and copper supplements: add known dietary and supplemental exposures rather than treating “liver” as pharmacologically separate. Where a desiccated product lacks quantitative nutrient assays, exact combined exposure is unknown.

## Quality, contamination, processing, and regulation

Processing affects microbiology and composition. Cooking reduces microbial risk when adequate internal temperatures are achieved but changes moisture and therefore concentration per gram. Desiccation or freeze-drying concentrates solids but is not, by itself, proof of a validated pathogen-kill step. A dried organ product requires product-specific process controls rather than an assumption that “dry” means sterile.

Environmental contaminants are source-dependent. A Brazilian study analyzed 250 cattle/chicken muscle and liver samples and quantified arsenic, cadmium and lead. Estimated liver-derived exposures were relatively low compared with the study's benchmarks, but the study confirmed that these elements can occur in cattle and chicken liver. [R19]

A separate Iranian assessment of cow and sheep meat/offal detected cadmium, chromium, copper, nickel and lead and found species/tissue differences; lead generated the largest target-hazard-quotient contribution. These surveillance results establish variability and the need for sourcing/testing, not that every liver product is contaminated. [R20]

SISTIK illustrates unusually good trial-level product characterization. Investigators analyzed batches at baseline, midline and endline; tested a one-year shelf life; reported Pb, Hg, Cd, As and Sn as not detected in tested crackers; reported Salmonella negative; and measured specified microbial counts. The testing laboratory was ISO/IEC 17025-accredited. Those data apply only to SISTIK lots and cannot be borrowed by unrelated chicken-liver foods or supplements. [R6]

For a desiccated liver/tissue product, a useful minimum batch record includes species and tissue identity, finished-product serving mass, raw-to-dried ratio or residual moisture, quantitative retinol/vitamin-A, copper and iron assays, B12/folate where claimed, Pb/Cd and preferably As/Hg testing, microbiological results, lot/date identifiers, and clarity about whether results are finished-product rather than raw-material tests. “Freeze-dried,” “grass-fed” or similar descriptors do not supply those measurements.

In the United States, capsules marketed as dietary supplements fall under the dietary-supplement/food regulatory framework, including 21 CFR Part 111 current good manufacturing practice and serious-adverse-event reporting requirements. This framework is not FDA approval of efficacy for energy, cognition, anemia, testosterone, liver disease or “detox” claims. [R21]

No persuasive liver-powder-specific pharmacovigilance signal with a reliable exposure denominator was identified. That is an evidence gap, not affirmative evidence of long-term safety; spontaneous adverse-event systems cannot establish a safe chronic retinol/copper exposure in poorly characterized products.
