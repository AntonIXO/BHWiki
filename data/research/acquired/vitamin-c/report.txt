Vitamin C — Deep Research Report and BHWiki Source
Executive summary

Vitamin C is L-ascorbic acid/ascorbate, an essential water-soluble micronutrient with formula C₆H₈O₆, molecular weight 176.12 g/mol, and PubChem CID 54670067. Its most secure biological roles are as a reducing cofactor/electron donor in collagen-related hydroxylation and other biosynthetic reactions, as part of cellular redox chemistry, and as an enhancer of non-heme iron absorption. Severe sustained deficiency causes scurvy. 
1

The central interpretive distinction is deficiency correction versus additional supplementation in vitamin-C-replete people. A classic controlled depletion study found that the five volunteers who completed prolonged deprivation developed full clinical scurvy after roughly 84–97 days; the syndrome included hemorrhagic/connective-tissue manifestations and reversed with vitamin C repletion. That evidence establishes essentiality and deficiency treatment, but does not establish benefit from pharmacologic supplementation in people who already have adequate status. 
2

Oral and intravenous vitamin C are pharmacologically different interventions. In a controlled human pharmacokinetic study, 1.25 g orally produced a mean peak plasma concentration of about 134.8 µmol/L, versus 885 µmol/L intravenously at the same dose; modeling predicted a ceiling near 220 µmol/L even with maximally tolerated repeated oral dosing, whereas pharmacologic IV administration can reach millimolar concentrations. Oral bioavailability falls as doses rise because intestinal uptake, tissue saturation, renal reabsorption, and urinary loss are tightly regulated. 
3
 The current U.S. ASCOR label reports a mean half-life of 7.4 ± 1.4 h after a 1,000 mg IV infusion in a small PK subset, whereas a modern high-dose IV study reported mean terminal estimates around 1.9 h in healthy volunteers and 2.4 h in oncology participants, illustrating the dose/model dependence of any single “half-life.” 
4
 
5

For ordinary nutrition, U.S. adult RDAs are 90 mg/day for men and 75 mg/day for women, rising to 85 mg/day in adult pregnancy and 120 mg/day in adult lactation; smokers are advised an additional 35 mg/day. The U.S. adult tolerable upper intake level is 2,000 mg/day. WHO/FAO uses a different derivation and recommends 45 mg/day for adults, 55 mg/day during pregnancy, and 70 mg/day during lactation, illustrating that nutrient-reference systems are not interchangeable. 
6

For the common cold, the best-established pattern remains nuanced: in a Cochrane review, regular vitamin C produced essentially no general-population incidence reduction (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–1.00; 10,708 participants), but the subgroup exposed to brief severe physical stress—marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers exercising in subarctic conditions—had lower incidence (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.35–0.64; n=598). Regular supplementation shortened cold duration by about 8% in adults and 14% in children, whereas starting vitamin C after symptoms began produced no consistent duration or severity effect. The extreme-stress result is not evidence of general exercise-performance enhancement. 
7

Large long-term prevention trials were convincingly null. In 14,641 male physicians, 500 mg/day oral vitamin C for about eight years did not reduce major cardiovascular events (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.89–1.11) and did not reduce total cancer (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92–1.10). 
8
 
9

The critical-care evidence argues against routine pharmacologic IV vitamin C for sepsis. CITRIS-ALI did not improve its prespecified modified-SOFA primary outcome (mean difference −0.10, 95% CI −1.23 to 1.03). 
10
 More importantly, the much larger blinded LOVIT trial found death or persistent organ dysfunction at day 28 in 44.5% versus 38.5%, RR 1.21 (95% CI 1.04–1.40), with vitamin C versus placebo. 
11
 The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign consequently suggests against IV vitamin C in adults with sepsis or septic shock, with low certainty of evidence. 
12

High-dose IV vitamin C in oncology remains investigational rather than a general anticancer treatment. The phase III VITALITY study in 442 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer did not significantly improve overall-population progression-free survival (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.70–1.05). 
13
 A randomized placebo-controlled prostate-cancer phase II trial likewise found no meaningful clinical benefit and stopped for futility. 
14
 Conversely, a very small randomized pancreatic-cancer trial reported longer survival with 75 g IV ascorbate three times weekly added to chemotherapy (OS HR 0.46, 90% CI 0.23–0.92; 36 randomized, 34 treated), an interesting signal that requires independent confirmation rather than extrapolation across tumors. 
15
 The U.S. National Cancer Institute states that FDA has not approved high-dose IV vitamin C as a cancer treatment. 
16

Safety depends strongly on exposure and route. High oral intake commonly causes gastrointestinal intolerance; pharmacologic or prolonged IV exposure raises distinct concerns about oxalate nephropathy/nephrolithiasis, severe hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, and oxidation-reduction assay interference. The current ASCOR prescribing information specifically addresses those risks and lists no formal contraindications, while requiring caution and monitoring in susceptible populations. 
4
 Vitamin C's enhancement of iron absorption also makes high-dose exposure particularly relevant in pathological iron-overload states. 
6

Two pharmacologic points prevent common interpretive errors. First, vitamin C is subject to concentration-dependent renal handling rather than simple dose-proportional oral kinetics: once tissues and plasma approach saturation, additional absorbed vitamin C is increasingly excreted. 
18
 Second, high-dose IV exposure can largely clear through the kidneys within 24 hours; in the 2022 pharmacokinetic study, urinary recovery approached 99% at 100 g in healthy volunteers, while oncology participants showed somewhat lower recovery. 
5

At the biochemical level, ascorbate's established roles include collagen-related enzyme chemistry, antioxidant/electron-donor functions, and increased non-heme iron absorption; these functions explain deficiency pathology but do not make an antioxidant biomarker or mechanistic effect equivalent to clinical efficacy. 
6
 Pharmacologic IV oncology hypotheses are additionally route-specific: the NCI review describes millimolar IV exposure and a proposed extracellular pro-oxidant mechanism that cannot be reproduced by ordinary oral dosing. 
16

## Summary

Vitamin C is the essential water-soluble micronutrient L-ascorbic acid/ascorbate. Its best-established human roles are as an electron donor and enzyme cofactor, especially in collagen hydroxylation, carnitine and catecholamine-related biosynthesis, antioxidant redox chemistry, and enhancement of non-heme iron absorption. Severe depletion causes scurvy; replacing deficiency is a qualitatively different evidence question from adding pharmacologic doses to vitamin-C-replete people.

Oral pharmacokinetics are strongly homeostatically controlled: intestinal absorption is saturable, tissue and plasma concentrations plateau, and renal excretion rises as intake increases. Intravenous administration bypasses intestinal control and can create millimolar pharmacologic concentrations that are not achievable by oral supplementation. These routes must not be treated as interchangeable interventions.

For people without deficiency, routine high-dose oral supplementation has not shown broad prevention of cardiovascular disease or cancer. Regular vitamin C does not materially lower common-cold incidence in the general population, although pooled trials show a modest reduction in cold duration and a larger incidence reduction in people exposed to brief severe physical stress. Starting vitamin C only after cold symptoms begin has not shown a consistent benefit. High-dose IV vitamin C is not established as routine therapy for sepsis or cancer: the large LOVIT sepsis trial found more death or persistent organ dysfunction, and 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance suggests against IV vitamin C; randomized oncology trials are heterogeneous, with major negative studies alongside a small positive pancreatic-cancer trial.

## Description

L-ascorbic acid has molecular formula C6H8O6 and molecular weight 176.12 g/mol. In physiologic solution it exists substantially as ascorbate. Common supplemental formulations include free ascorbic acid and mineral ascorbates such as sodium or calcium ascorbate; formulation claims should not be assumed to confer clinical superiority without product-specific evidence.

Humans cannot synthesize vitamin C and obtain it from diet. Fruits and vegetables are the principal sources; high-content examples include peppers, citrus fruit and juice, kiwifruit, broccoli, strawberries and Brussels sprouts. Storage and heat can reduce food vitamin C content. U.S. adult RDAs are 90 mg/day for men and 75 mg/day for women, with pregnancy and lactation adjustments, and smokers are advised an additional 35 mg/day. The adult U.S. tolerable upper intake level is 2,000 mg/day. WHO/FAO RNIs are lower (45 mg/day for adults, 55 mg/day in pregnancy and 70 mg/day in lactation), illustrating that reference values differ by authority and derivation method.

Scurvy reflects failure of vitamin-C-dependent connective-tissue biology. Controlled depletion experiments and clinical observations describe fatigue followed by follicular hyperkeratosis, perifollicular or other hemorrhage, bruising, painful/swollen joints, gingival swelling and bleeding, poor wound healing and anemia. In a classic tightly controlled depletion study, full clinical scurvy appeared after roughly 84–97 days of a vitamin-C-free diet in the remaining five volunteers, when body stores had fallen markedly; real-world timelines vary with starting stores, intake and comorbidity.

## Evidence note

Evidence is strongest for prevention and correction of deficiency and for the route-dependent pharmacokinetic behavior of ascorbate. Evidence for additional supplementation is indication-specific. A biomarker or plasma concentration change is not itself a clinical benefit, and results obtained with pharmacologic IV concentrations cannot be extrapolated to oral supplementation.

The common-cold literature supports a small duration effect from regular prophylactic intake, not meaningful prevention in the general population; the extreme-exercise subgroup signal should not be generalized to ordinary exercise performance. Large long-duration randomized trials in well-nourished men found no cardiovascular or cancer-prevention benefit from 500 mg/day oral vitamin C. In critical illness, the large LOVIT trial raised a harm signal and current sepsis guidance recommends against routine IV vitamin C. In oncology, positive and negative randomized trials coexist, differ by cancer type and regimen, and do not establish a general anticancer indication. Deficiency correction and pharmacologic cancer/sepsis dosing therefore require separate interpretation.

## Mechanisms

```yaml
- title: Collagen hydroxylation and connective-tissue integrity
  description: Vitamin C acts as a reducing cofactor for iron-dependent prolyl and lysyl hydroxylation reactions required for normal collagen maturation. Severe deficiency therefore impairs connective tissue, explaining capillary fragility, gingival disease and poor wound healing in scurvy.
  sourceId: ref-ods
  conceptId: collagen-hydroxylation
- title: Redox electron donor and antioxidant network
  description: Ascorbate donates electrons in aqueous compartments and can quench reactive oxidants; it also participates in recycling other antioxidants. Antioxidant chemistry is a biochemical role and does not by itself establish benefit from high-dose supplementation for chronic disease.
  sourceId: ref-ods
  conceptId: ascorbate-redox
- title: Enhancement of non-heme iron absorption
  description: Ascorbate improves non-heme iron bioavailability, in part by maintaining iron in a more soluble reduced state in the intestinal lumen. The magnitude is meal- and diet-dependent, so a single-meal absorption effect should not be treated as a universal long-term change in iron stores.
  sourceId: ref-ods
  conceptId: nonheme-iron-absorption
- title: Saturable transport and renal homeostasis
  description: Sodium-dependent vitamin C transport supports intestinal uptake, tissue accumulation and renal reclamation. As doses rise, oral fractional absorption falls, plasma concentrations plateau and urinary excretion increases; dehydroascorbate can also enter cells through glucose-transport pathways before intracellular reduction.
  sourceId: ref-levine-1996
  conceptId: ascorbate-transport-homeostasis
- title: Pharmacologic IV ascorbate as a context-dependent pro-oxidant
  description: Intravenous dosing can create millimolar extracellular ascorbate concentrations that are unattainable orally. Preclinical cancer models support extracellular hydrogen-peroxide-generating pro-oxidant chemistry under pharmacologic conditions, but this mechanism does not establish clinical efficacy and is tumor- and regimen-dependent.
  sourceId: ref-nci-pdq
  conceptId: pharmacologic-ascorbate-prooxidant
```

## Cautions

```yaml
- title: Gastrointestinal intolerance at high oral intakes
  description: Gram-range oral vitamin C commonly causes osmotic gastrointestinal effects such as diarrhea, nausea and abdominal cramps; the U.S. adult UL is 2,000 mg/day and is not a target intake.
  sourceId: ref-ods
- title: Oxalate nephropathy and nephrolithiasis risk
  description: Ascorbate is metabolized in part to oxalate. The current ASCOR label reports acute and chronic oxalate nephropathy with prolonged high-dose exposure and warns that renal impairment, a history of oxalate stones and older age can increase risk. Case reports and observational signals do not establish a single incidence estimate for all oral or IV exposures.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
- title: G6PD deficiency and high-dose intravenous exposure
  description: Severe hemolysis has been reported after ascorbic acid administration in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. The ASCOR label requires dose reduction and blood-count monitoring in this population; pharmacologic oncology trials commonly exclude G6PD deficiency.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
- title: Iron-overload disorders
  description: Vitamin C can increase non-heme iron absorption and can mobilize redox-active iron. High-dose supplementation warrants particular caution in hereditary hemochromatosis or other iron-overload states; evidence in iron-replete people should not be extrapolated to these populations.
  sourceId: ref-ods
- title: Oxidation-reduction laboratory interference
  description: High circulating ascorbate can interfere with oxidation-reduction-based laboratory methods. The ASCOR label specifically warns about blood or urine glucose testing and assays for nitrite, bilirubin and leukocytes and advises delaying susceptible testing until 24 hours after infusion when possible.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
- title: Oral and intravenous evidence are not interchangeable
  description: Oral dosing is constrained by saturable absorption and renal homeostasis, whereas IV dosing produces pharmacologic plasma concentrations. Safety and efficacy claims from one route cannot be assumed for the other.
  sourceId: ref-padayatty-2004
```

## Interactions

```yaml
- id: interaction-vitamin-c-iron
  name: Iron absorption and iron-overload states
  otherSlug: null
  summary: Vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption; high supplemental or pharmacologic exposure can be undesirable in people with pathologic iron overload.
  sourceId: ref-ods
  mechanism: Reduces and solubilizes non-heme iron, increasing intestinal availability; redox-active iron can also alter oxidative chemistry.
  context: Particularly relevant to hereditary hemochromatosis and other iron-overload disorders.
- id: interaction-vitamin-c-lab-redox
  name: Oxidation-reduction laboratory assays
  otherSlug: null
  summary: High circulating ascorbate can generate inaccurate results in susceptible blood and urine assays, including some glucose methods.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
  mechanism: Ascorbic acid is a strong reducing agent that interferes with colorimetric or electrochemical oxidation-reduction reactions.
  context: The ASCOR label advises postponing susceptible tests until 24 hours after infusion when possible.
- id: interaction-vitamin-c-warfarin
  name: Warfarin anticoagulation
  otherSlug: null
  summary: The ASCOR label notes limited case reports suggesting interference, but reports no effect in patients receiving up to 1,000 mg/day ascorbic acid for two weeks; standard anticoagulation monitoring should continue.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
  mechanism: Not established.
  context: Evidence is limited and does not justify assuming a clinically important interaction in every patient.
- id: interaction-vitamin-c-urine-ph
  name: Drugs affected by urine acidification
  otherSlug: null
  summary: The ASCOR label states that ascorbic acid can acidify urine and thereby alter renal handling of pH-sensitive drugs, including lowering amphetamine concentrations.
  sourceId: ref-ascor-label
  mechanism: Urine acidification can change ionization and renal excretion of susceptible drugs.
  context: Label-specific warning for intravenous ascorbic acid; clinical relevance depends on exposure and the co-administered drug.
```
