## Summary

**Executive summary.** Zeaxanthin is the dietary `(3R,3'R)` xanthophyll concentrated with lutein and meso-zeaxanthin in human macular pigment. Its molecular identity is well established, but the clinical evidence for **standalone zeaxanthin supplementation** is much smaller and more internally conflicting than the literature on combined lutein/zeaxanthin formulations.

The clearest standalone retinal-pigment evidence conflicts. The LUXEA program used identically formulated lutein or zeaxanthin and reported that zeaxanthin produced similar pigment accumulation at foveal and parafoveal locations, which initially confounded differential heterochromatic-flicker MPOD measurement; after correction, the investigators estimated an approximately **14% MPOD increase**. In contrast, a randomized study of 22 healthy Japanese volunteers found that **10 mg/day zeaxanthin for up to 3 months left resonance-Raman MPOD essentially unchanged**, while 10 mg/day lutein increased MPOD by more than 20% at months 2 and 3.

The principal standalone disease trial, the Zeaxanthin and Visual Function Study (ZVF; NCT00564902), randomized 60 predominantly male participants with mild-to-moderate atrophic AMD to 8 mg/day zeaxanthin, zeaxanthin plus lutein, or a **9 mg/day lutein active control rather than an inert placebo**. Mean MPOD across the three groups increased from 0.33 ± 0.17 to 0.51 ± 0.18 density units at 12 months (within-trial P=0.03), but there was **no significant between-group difference (P=0.47)**. The zeaxanthin arm improved high-contrast acuity by a reported 1.5 lines, while shape discrimination and central-scotoma outcomes were borderline; lutein performed better on low-contrast acuity, contrast sensitivity and glare recovery. These exploratory findings do not establish that zeaxanthin is superior to lutein or placebo.

A separate randomized open-label study in eight people with macular telangiectasia type 2 tested **10 or 20 mg/day standalone zeaxanthin for 24 months**. Zeaxanthin increased pigment in the pre-existing perifoveal ring but did not restore pigment to the depleted central fovea and produced **no objective visual-acuity, contrast-sensitivity or OCT benefit**. One participant receiving 20 mg/day developed reversible inner-retinal crystalline deposits after about six months; they disappeared after supplementation stopped.

Combination trials frequently show increased MPOD and sometimes better photostress recovery, chromatic contrast, glare-related performance or retinal electrophysiology, but those effects cannot be assigned to zeaxanthin alone. This includes trials using 10 mg lutein plus 2 mg zeaxanthin, 10 mg plus 10 mg, zeaxanthin/lutein products containing additional antioxidants, and AREDS2. AREDS2 specifically tested adding **10 mg lutein plus 2 mg zeaxanthin to an AREDS background formulation**; its primary comparison did not significantly reduce progression to advanced AMD (HR 0.90, 98.7% CI 0.76–1.07; P=0.12), so AREDS2 is not standalone zeaxanthin-efficacy evidence.

Overall, human trials support oral absorption and retinal incorporation of zeaxanthin, but the magnitude and spatial pattern of MPOD response depend on measurement method, formulation, retinal condition and co-supplements. Evidence for clinically meaningful standalone improvements in vision or AMD progression remains limited. The small MacTel safety signal argues against treating the absence of common adverse events in other small trials as proof that high-dose long-term zeaxanthin is risk-free.

## Description

Zeaxanthin is a C40 xanthophyll carotenoid with molecular formula C40H56O2, molecular weight 568.9 g/mol and PubChem CID 5280899. The naturally occurring dietary stereoisomer addressed here is `(3R,3'R)-zeaxanthin`; **meso-zeaxanthin `(3R,3'S)` is a distinct stereoisomer and combination studies containing meso-zeaxanthin are not treated as standalone zeaxanthin trials**.

The human ocular literature is unusually sensitive to measurement method. Psychophysical heterochromatic flicker photometry estimates macular pigment from differential short-wavelength sensitivity, whereas resonance Raman spectroscopy measures carotenoid Raman signal and autofluorescence methods infer pigment from attenuation of fundus autofluorescence. LUXEA is particularly important because zeaxanthin accumulated at both the foveal measurement location and its parafoveal reference, reducing the apparent HFP difference and requiring correction.

## Evidence note

Standalone zeaxanthin evidence should not be pooled conceptually with lutein/zeaxanthin or lutein/zeaxanthin/meso-zeaxanthin products. LUXEA and the Tanito trial directly conflict on whether conventional central MPOD measurably rises with zeaxanthin alone, although their measurement approaches, populations and durations differ. ZVF provides exploratory visual-function evidence in AMD but used lutein as its control and was commercially supported. The MacTel study provides important negative and safety evidence: an anatomic pigment response did not translate into improved objective vision or OCT structure.

AREDS2 is retained only as context for AMD progression and combination evidence. All participants were generally taking an AREDS antioxidant/zinc background formula, and the tested carotenoid intervention was 10 mg lutein plus 2 mg zeaxanthin. The primary addition analysis was not statistically significant for advanced AMD; therefore it cannot establish either standalone zeaxanthin efficacy or a zeaxanthin-specific dose effect.

## Mechanisms

```yaml
- title: "Selective retinal accumulation into macular pigment"
  description: "Human supplementation studies show that oral zeaxanthin can accumulate in retinal macular-pigment regions. LUXEA found zeaxanthin deposition at both foveal and parafoveal locations, while the MacTel trial showed that deposition depended strongly on pre-existing retinal pigment geography and did not repopulate pigment-depleted central fovea."
  sourceId: ref-luxea-2007
  conceptId: macular-pigment-accumulation

- title: "Short-wavelength light filtering by macular pigment"
  description: "Zeaxanthin is a constituent of macular pigment that absorbs short-wavelength visible light. Human visual trials test the downstream hypothesis that increasing macular pigment can alter glare, chromatic contrast or photostress performance; these trials demonstrate associations and intervention effects on some visual endpoints but do not prove that filtering is the sole causal mechanism."
  sourceId: ref-hammond-2014
  conceptId: short-wavelength-light-filtering
```

## Cautions

```yaml
- title: "Reversible crystalline maculopathy reported at 20 mg/day"
  description: "In the eight-person MacTel study, one participant receiving 20 mg/day zeaxanthin developed yellowish inner-retinal crystals after approximately six months. The deposits disappeared within months after supplementation stopped, and visual acuity was not measurably reduced by the crystals. A single case cannot establish incidence but is important high-dose safety evidence."
  sourceId: ref-mactel-2017

- title: "Standalone efficacy is substantially less certain than combination evidence"
  description: "Many positive human studies used lutein plus zeaxanthin, meso-zeaxanthin, omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants or multinutrient formulations. Those trials cannot identify zeaxanthin's independent effect and should not be presented as standalone zeaxanthin efficacy."
  sourceId: ref-hammond-2014

- title: "Standalone MPOD trials conflict"
  description: "LUXEA estimated an approximately 14% corrected MPOD increase with zeaxanthin, whereas the randomized Japanese study found no central MPOD increase with 10 mg/day zeaxanthin over three months. Differences in retinal spatial sampling, measurement method, duration and population may contribute, but the conflict remains unresolved."
  sourceId: ref-tanito-2012

- title: "ZVF did not use an inert placebo"
  description: "The ZVF publication is described as placebo-controlled in its title, but the registry and paper identify the control as 9 mg/day lutein ('faux placebo'). Improvements within the 8 mg/day zeaxanthin arm therefore do not constitute a zeaxanthin-versus-inert-placebo effect estimate."
  sourceId: ref-zvf-registry

- title: "AREDS2 is not standalone zeaxanthin evidence"
  description: "AREDS2 tested 10 mg lutein plus 2 mg zeaxanthin, generally on top of an AREDS antioxidant/zinc formulation. Its primary advanced-AMD comparison was not statistically significant, and neither the design nor formulation isolates zeaxanthin."
  sourceId: ref-areds2-2013

- title: "Meso-zeaxanthin is not interchangeable with dietary zeaxanthin"
  description: "Meso-zeaxanthin is a distinct stereoisomer. Trials of lutein/zeaxanthin/meso-zeaxanthin formulations are combination-product evidence and are not counted here as standalone (3R,3'R)-zeaxanthin efficacy."
  sourceId: ref-pubchem-zeaxanthin
```

Human studies estimate it with methods including heterochromatic flicker photometry, resonance Raman spectroscopy and fundus autofluorescence; values and spatial interpretation are method-dependent.

## Description

Human supplementation studies support a directed link from oral zeaxanthin exposure to retinal macular-pigment accumulation and from that accumulation to MPOD measurements. LUXEA also demonstrates an important measurement caveat: zeaxanthin can accumulate at both the foveal target and parafoveal reference location, so a differential HFP value can underestimate accumulation unless the spatial response is considered.

Coverage and access gaps

The high-confidence standalone evidence base captured here consists chiefly of LUXEA, ZVF, the randomized Japanese lutein-versus-zeaxanthin study, and the MacTel dose study. LUXEA’s corrected response supports retinal accumulation, but its HFP reference location was itself affected by zeaxanthin; Tanito et al. found no central MPOD response to 10 mg/day over three months; and MacTel showed that increasing pigment outside the foveal center need not improve acuity, contrast sensitivity, or OCT structure. 3

The strongest safety-specific primary observation found for standalone high-dose zeaxanthin was the single reversible crystalline maculopathy case during 20 mg/day treatment in the eight-person MacTel study. The authors explicitly reported no proprietary conflicts, while funding included NIH, Research to Prevent Blindness, the Lowy Medical Research Institute, and in-kind zeaxanthin from ZeaVision. This is a signal, not an incidence estimate. 4

Funding and conflict fields remain not-assessed wherever an explicit original-paper declaration was not accessible or inspected; corporate author affiliation was deliberately not converted into an industry-funding classification. For ZVF, commercial sponsorship could be verified independently: Chrysantis was the primary granting sponsor, alongside VA/public support and several commercial instrumentation contributors. 5

AREDS2 is intentionally not represented as evidence that zeaxanthin alone prevents AMD progression. It randomized a lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg combination on an AREDS background; the primary advanced-AMD comparison was HR 0.90 with a 98.7% CI of 0.76–1.07 and P=0.12. The U.S. legal records are likewise narrowly scoped: FDA's GRAS letters say the agency had no questions regarding the notifiers' conclusions for specified food uses, while explicitly distinguishing that response from FDA itself making a GRAS determination or approving therapeutic eye-disease claims. 6
