### Abstract

> Calcium (Ca²⁺) signaling is essential for neurotransmitter release, synaptic plasticity, gene expression, and neuronal excitability. Nicotine, the addictive component of tobacco, activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), including alpha4ß2- and a7-containing receptor subtypes, thereby altering neuronal Ca²⁺ entry. ATP2B2 encodes plasma membrane calcium-transporting ATPase 2 (PMCA2), a neuronal high-affinity Ca²⁺ extrusion pump that helps shape local Ca²⁺ microdomains. Published work has shown that PMCA2 functionally associates with a7-nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (alpha7-nAChRs) through postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95) and can influence a7-nAChR surface expression. Genetic and transcriptomic studies have also linked ATP2B2-related pathways to substance use phenotypes, but there is no direct evidence that chronic nicotine alters PMCA2 expression. This narrative review distinguishes established findings from indirect evidence and untested predictions. We propose a mechanistic hypothesis in which chronic nicotine exposure may establish a new calcium-homeostatic equilibrium characterized by alpha7-nAChR downregulation and compensatory PMCA2 upregulation. We also hypothesize that, during early withdrawal, persistently elevated PMCA2 activity could contribute to excessive Ca²⁺ extrusion, reduced presynaptic glutamate release, cognitive impairment, and relapse vulnerability. These predicted effects require direct testing in nicotine exposure and withdrawal models. This review aims to examine the roles of calcium signaling and homeostasis in nicotine addiction, with particular emphasis on ATP2B2, which encodes the calcium ion pump PMCA2, and alpha7-nAChR.
