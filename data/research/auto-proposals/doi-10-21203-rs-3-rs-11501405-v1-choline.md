### Abstract

> <title>Abstract</title> <p> Background <italic>Acanthamoeba</italic> brain infection can cause ring-enhancing lesions that mimic neoplasm, abscess or demyelination on brain imaging. High-dose corticosteroids, the standard treatment for tumefactive demyelination, can be detrimental if infection is not systematically excluded. Case presentation: A 42-year-old immunocompetent woman presented with a two-week history of non-fluent aphasia, executive dysfunction, and progressive right hemiparesis. An MRI scan of the brain showed two left frontoparietal lesions with perilesional oedema, open-ring enhancement, and mass effect. Spectroscopy showed elevated choline and reduced N-acetyl aspartate. Based on the radiological differential diagnosis of tumefactive demyelination versus high-grade glioma, intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily was initiated at an outside hospital before referral to our centre. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) study on day 5 of methylprednisolone showed 6 cells/mm³, sugar 78 mg/dL, protein 79 mg/dL, and no oligoclonal bands. CSF 18S rRNA PCR detected <italic>Acanthamoeba spp.</italic> Combination anti-acanthamoeba therapy (doxycycline, fluconazole, co-trimoxazole, and miltefosine) was administered. The lesions regressed, and deficits improved; however, CSF <italic>Acanthamoeba</italic> PCR remained positive even after four months of treatment, with co-detection of <italic>parvovirus</italic> B19. Conclusion <italic>Acanthamoeba</italic> CNS infection can mimic the clinical, neuroradiological, and spectroscopic features of tumefactive demyelination, including the characteristic open-ring sign. When tumefactive lesions lack definitive features of established demyelinating disease, it is prudent to rule out infective etiologies, such as CNS infections with free-living amoebae, before administering immunomodulators. </p>
