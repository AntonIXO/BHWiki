### Abstract

> Exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD) initiates a coordinated response in which redox signaling and innate immunity jointly shape tissue clearance, inflammatory resolution, and muscle regeneration. Oxidants generated by contracting myofibres, vascular and stromal cells, and recruited leukocytes can amplify membrane and molecular injury when insufficiently controlled, but they also regulate immune-cell recruitment, phagocytosis, efferocytosis, satellite-cell activity, and adaptive remodeling. This critical review integrates evidence on the temporal and spatial crosstalk among reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, endogenous antioxidant systems, and the regenerative niche after EIMD. Human studies document post-exercise changes in circulating leukocytes and intramuscular immune markers, yet direct cell-specific measurements of redox activity and regeneration remain scarce; most causal mechanisms derive from animal and cellular experiments. The review proposes a time-resolved redox–immune relay encompassing damage sensing, neutrophil-dominant amplification and clearance, monocyte/macrophage reprogramming, inflammatory resolution, and tissue remodeling. Macrophage behavior is treated as a dynamic multidimensional continuum rather than a binary M1/M2 sequence. Seven falsifiable propositions operationalize the framework. They predict that antioxidant effects depend on dose, timing, chemical target, tissue compartment, baseline redox status, and injury severity, and that improvements in soreness, creatine kinase, cytokines, or bulk oxidative markers are insufficient to establish enhanced regeneration. Available evidence does not support indiscriminate suppression of post-exercise oxidation. Instead, interventions should be evaluated by their capacity to constrain pathological oxidation while preserving redox-dependent immune and regenerative signaling.
