### Abstract

> <b>Background:</b> Sleep deprivation impairs cognitive performance and may alter regional cerebral oxygenation. Nutritional strategies that preserve brain oxygenation could help mitigate these adverse effects. <b>Aim:</b> To compare the acute effects of creatine-guanidinoacetate (GAA), molecular hydrogen inhalation, caffeine, and placebo on regional brain oxygenation and attention-related performance following sleep deprivation.<b>Methods:</b> Twenty-two healthy young adults completed a 24-hour sleep deprivation protocol and were randomly assigned to receive creatine-GAA, molecular hydrogen inhalation, caffeine, or placebo. Brain oxygenation was assessed using functional near-infrared spectroscopy over the frontal, temporal, and occipital cortices during a modified attention network test. Alertness, executive control, orienting, reaction time, and accuracy were also evaluated.<b>Summary:</b> Creatine-GAA significantly increased frontal oxygen saturation (50.8% ± 4.1% to 61.0% ± 5.9%, <i>P</i> = 0.01; Cohen's <i>d</i> = 2.00) and temporal oxygen saturation (63.1% ± 4.8% to 70.2% ± 2.6%, <i>P</i> = 0.01; Cohen's <i>d</i> = 1.83). Significant time-by-treatment interactions were observed for frontal oxygen saturation (<i>P</i> = 0.043; ηp<sup>2</sup> = 0.331), temporal oxygen saturation (<i>P</i> = 0.021; ηp<sup>2</sup> = 0.428), and occipital total hemoglobin (<i>P</i> = 0.022; ηp<sup>2</sup> = 0.405). No significant between-group differences were observed in attention-related outcomes. Acute creatine-GAA supplementation improved regional brain oxygenation following sleep deprivation, particularly in the frontal and temporal cortices, without measurable improvements in attention-related performance. These preliminary findings support further investigation of creatine-based nutritional strategies in larger randomized controlled trials.
